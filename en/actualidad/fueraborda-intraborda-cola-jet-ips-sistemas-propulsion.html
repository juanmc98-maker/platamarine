Outboard, inboard, sterndrive, jet or IPS: which propulsion system to choose

By Juan Morante21 September 20268 min read

When people start looking at boats, they almost always notice the length, the deck layout or the price first. The propulsion system tends to take a back seat, and yet it's what most shapes maintenance, fuel use, handling in the marina and what you'll spend every season. These are the real differences between the five systems you'll come across on the used market.

Outboard: the most common, and for good reason

The whole engine hangs outside the hull, on the transom. It's the dominant system on runabouts, RIBs and boats up to 9-10 metres, and it's showing up on ever larger boats thanks to the big-displacement engines Mercury, Yamaha and Suzuki have brought out in recent years.

Pros: cheaper, more accessible maintenance; the engine can be replaced or repowered without touching the hull; the whole cockpit stays free because there's no engine room; and if it fails it can be repaired or swapped relatively quickly. The downside: above a certain power, two or three big engines on the transom push up both purchase and maintenance costs, and there's more noise at the stern than with a well-insulated inboard.

Sterndrive: the middle ground

The engine sits inside the hull, but the drive comes out through the stern on a steerable leg, as on Volvo Penta Duoprop or Mercruiser units. It's common on 8 to 14 metre cruisers and fishing/day boats.

In return it has more parts that can cause trouble (bellows, gimbal, trim system) and maintenance costs more than an equivalent outboard. It's worth checking the bellows and the leg's gearcase at every annual service; it's the most expensive failure if neglected.

Shaft-drive inboard: the classic solution

The engine is fixed inside the hull and drives the propeller through a straight shaft that runs through the hull. It's the most traditional system on fishing boats and on sailing yachts with an auxiliary engine, and it's still common on cruisers over 10-11 metres.

Its weak point is handling: with no steerable propeller, manoeuvring into a tight berth depends a lot on the skipper's skill, which is why large boats of this type so often come with a bow thruster fitted as standard.

Jet drive: quiet and very shallow draft

Instead of a propeller, the engine drives an impeller that draws water in and forces it out through a nozzle at the stern. It's the system used on jet skis and on more and more RIBs and small boats, especially in areas with shallow water, rocks or coves with little depth.

The big advantage is that nothing sticks out of the hull, so the risk of hitting something underwater is much lower, and low-speed handling is excellent. The drawbacks are weaker performance at low revs compared with a conventional propeller, and that any seaweed or sand can clog the water intake.

IPS and pod drives: the high end, and why they cost what they cost

Volvo Penta's IPS systems (or their equivalents from other brands) carry the propellers on steerable pods under the hull, facing forward rather than aft like a traditional sterndrive. You mainly see them on mid-to-high-end cruisers and flybridges from 11-12 metres.

What sets it apart from a classic inboard is fuel use (lower, thanks to counter-rotating propellers working in clean water) and, above all, handling in the marina. That's why "joystick" has become such a common selling point in high-end used boat listings in recent years. The flip side: only the brand's authorised workshops service it and parts are more expensive. Before buying a used boat with IPS, always ask for the service history of the drive system, not just the engine.

How I'd decide

For day trips along the coast up to 9-10 metres, a well-maintained outboard is still the most practical and cheapest option to keep running. For a 10 to 13 metre cruiser, a shaft drive with a bow thruster gives long-term peace of mind. If you'll be using it in shallow coves or over sensitive seabeds, jet wins by default. And if the budget stretches to a high-end flybridge, IPS with a joystick takes the fear out of manoeuvring in a marina full of people watching.

Either way, before buying always ask for the maintenance history of the propulsion system, not just the engine: on an inboard or an IPS, that's usually where the nasty surprise you can't see at first glance is hiding.
