Which outboard engine to buy in 2026

By Juan Morante10 September 20269 min read

Every season people ask me the same thing when they're re-powering or buying a boat with a new engine: Yamaha or Mercury? Is the Suzuki worth it? What about electric? I've put together what's on the market this year, with official manufacturer data, so the decision gets made with numbers rather than habit or what the boat next door has.

First things first: I don't sell engines and I'm not paid by any brand. What follows is what I look at myself when I value a boat or advise a buyer, and the data comes from each manufacturer's official pages and public price lists. Sources are at the end of the article.

What's new this year

2026 isn't a year of revolution in horsepower, but it is one for electronics and a couple of interesting new engines in the mid-range.

  • Tohatsu MFS150A. The most significant new product for 6–8 metre boats: the first 150hp developed entirely in-house by Tohatsu (until now their bigger engines came from Honda). Four cylinders, 1,995 cc, 178.5 kg in its lightest version, 41A alternator and 20- and 25-inch shafts. Launched in January in Düsseldorf, with deliveries starting in April.
  • Mercury V10 Verado 425hp and the updated 350hp, with a 150A alternator, the largest in its class, and 26-inch engine spacing that makes it easier to swap two old engines for two new ones without touching the transom. Mercury also showed a 808hp V12, but that's a concept with no release date.
  • Yamaha hasn't released new horsepower: its focus for 2026 is the Helm Master EX system, now with a wireless joystick remote, a Dock Hold function to stay pinned to the pontoon, and integration with Sleipner and Lewmar bow thrusters. The 4.3-litre V6 F350, launched in 2024, remains its lightest engine at that power.
  • Suzuki is expanding the Stealth Line (matte black finish) in the US with the DF225 and the small 9.9 and 25hp; on Spain's 2026 price list the Stealth finish is available on the DF115, 140, 150, 200, 300 and 350. Since autumn 2025 the twin-prop DF350AMD and DF300BMD integrate with VETUS bow thrusters under a single joystick. There's talk of a new big Suzuki engine, but there's no official spec sheet yet, so I'm not covering it here.
  • Honda keeps the 5-litre V8 BF350 as its flagship; the M300 co-developed with McLaren is sold only in the US, with no European date.

Weight matters, sometimes more than the brand

On a used 6–9 metre boat, one of the mistakes I see most often when re-powering is fitting an engine heavier than the transom and flotation were designed for. Ten or twenty extra kilos show up in the stern, in trim and in how water comes in through the scuppers with people aft. These are the official dry weights published by each manufacturer:

And the alternator matters more every year: with a big plotter, fridge, windlass and bow thruster, the Verado V8's 115A or the Honda BF250's 90A aren't a luxury.

Warranties in Spain

Warranties vary by country, and what you read on US forums doesn't always apply in Spain. What each brand applies in Spain for private use, per their official documents:

  • Mercury (EMEA): 2 years on FourStroke engines from 2.5 to 150hp and 3 years on Verado V6, V8, V10 and V12, extendable up to 5 years with annual servicing at a dealer. Three years against corrosion.
  • Suzuki: 3 years with official service, per its 2026 price list. Some dealers advertise extensions, but those are promotional terms worth getting in writing.
  • Honda: 3 years for private use (1 year for professional use), registering the engine within 30 days of purchase.
  • Yamaha: 3 years from the factory, and dealers offer to extend to 5 with the official-service maintenance programme. That extension is transferable to the next owner, which is a real selling point on resale.
  • Tohatsu: dealers in Spain quote 3 years; worth confirming with the importer at the time of purchase.

Ballpark prices

Few brands publish a price list in Spain. Suzuki does: on its 2026 list, excluding VAT, the DF115B runs around €15,300–15,600, the DF150A €17,900–18,000, the DF200A €21,000–21,400, the DF250 €24,800–25,000, the DF300AP €29,100–29,600 and the DF350AMD €38,000–38,500. For Yamaha, prices shown by online stores put the F115 around €16,300, the F150 at €19,200 and the four-cylinder F200 at €23,000. A Mercury Verado 300 V8 with digital controls is advertised at a Costa Brava dealer from around €38,000 with VAT.

Always add what's not in the engine price: propeller, controls and cables or digital harness, instrumentation, installation, and in many cases a new battery. On a 150hp re-power it's easy for the total to run €2,000–4,000 above the engine price alone.

If you're re-powering a used boat

This is the situation I see most: a 2005–2012 boat in good condition with a 1,500-hour engine or a two-stroke nobody wants anymore. Before choosing a brand, four checks:

  1. Builder's plate. The maximum power and maximum engine weight the boat is rated for are on the CE plate. Exceeding them can cause problems with insurance and registration; worth checking beforehand.
  2. Transom. Height (short, long or extra-long shaft) and condition. A damp or soft transom shows up when you fit the new engine, and needs repairing first.
  3. Controls and steering. Current engines with digital controls don't reuse old cables; budget for controls, harness and, on bigger engines, hydraulic or electric steering. With electric steering, check batteries and wiring.
  4. Fuel. Replace hoses and fit a 10-micron water-separating fuel filter. It's cheap and prevents one of the most common first-year failures.

And one thing that affects value: a €60,000 boat with a new engine isn't worth €60,000 plus the engine. You usually recover part of it, and it typically sells faster. I cover this in the guide to how the price of a used boat is set.

What about electric?

For tenders and small boats it's already a serious option: the Yamaha Harmo (roughly equivalent to 10hp), Mercury Avator models up to the 110e (about 15hp equivalent), Tohatsu's electric outboard developed with Ilmor, and the Torqeedo and ePropulsion ranges, which are refreshing their 2–6kW models in 2026. For a 7–12 metre boat planing at 25 knots, not yet: the big electric motors exist (Evoy, Torqeedo Deep Blue, RAD), but the motor-plus-battery package costs and weighs far more than a petrol outboard, and range at planing speed is short. I also haven't found any public grant currently available in Catalonia in 2026 for electrifying recreational boats; there were some in the Balearics and the Valencian Community, but they're closed or limited to specific cases.

How I'd decide

If the boat is 5–7 metres with a tight transom, I'd look at weight first: the Tohatsu MFS150A and the 3-litre Mercurys are the lightest at that power, and the Suzuki 115s are very reasonable. If the boat is 8–12 metres with a lot of electronics, the alternator and the service network matter more: in my experience Yamaha and Mercury have the most workshops along the Catalan coast, Honda offers a lot of alternator capacity, Suzuki often comes out well on price sheet in hand, and Tohatsu is the light option at 150hp. And if you're thinking about swapping two engines, the 26-inch spacing on the Mercury V10s can save you touching the transom.

Either way, get a full quote (engine, propeller, controls, installation) from two workshops, and ask for the warranty in writing with its maintenance conditions. That's what I do before recommending anything.
