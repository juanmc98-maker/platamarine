Which boat to buy? A practical guide to choosing well

By Juan Morante11 September 20268 min read

To decide which boat to buy, I'd start with how you're going to use it and how much time you can give it each year. I come from the used-car trade and there's an idea I apply here too: the purchase price doesn't tell the whole story. The boat has to fit both when you're enjoying it and when it's time to maintain it.

The five questions before you look at listings

1. What will you really use it for?

Separate out swimming trips, fishing, lunch at anchor and sleeping aboard. They don't need the same layout. For the odd night, a small cabin might do; for a week, the berth, the heads, ventilation and where to stow luggage all matter.

Write down three must-haves and set the wish-list aside. If you'd only sleep aboard once a year, compare the cost of extra accommodation against just chartering a boat for that trip.

2. Who's usually on board?

Think about a normal day's crew, not every friend who might come along. Rated capacity doesn't guarantee comfort. Check shaded seating, walkways around the deck and access to the heads, especially with children, people with limited mobility or a dog.

Go aboard together before deciding. Try the boarding ladder, sit down to eat, step into the cabin. A useful layout can matter more than an extra half metre of length.

3. From where, and how many days a year?

A nearby marina makes it easy to head out for a few hours. A farther one can work for holidays, but adds travel time. Before committing to a boat, confirm mooring availability for its overall length and beam; the model's commercial name alone isn't enough.

Trailering and dry storage are alternatives, with limits on weight, access and hours. For trailering, check the documented combined weights, your car's towing capacity and the licence required. It's not decided by the boat's length alone.

4. What's your full budget?

Separate purchase, getting it up to scratch, and annual running costs. Add insurance, mooring, haul-out, maintenance, fuel and paperwork. Set money aside for an independent survey and for whatever it turns up; spending it all on the purchase leaves little room to enjoy the boat.

Taxation depends on the transaction. The Spanish registration tax (IEDMT) applies, with exceptions, to a recreational boat's first registration in Spain over 8 metres. It isn't paid again automatically on every resale. Check VAT or transfer tax (ITP) as applicable too, and see the guide to the registration tax.

5. Which licence do you have or need?

The Navigation Licence allows up to 6 metres, daytime only, within 2 nm of a port, marina or shelter. The PNB reaches 8 metres and 5 nm of shelter. The basic PER allows up to 15 metres and 12 nm from the coast, plus inter-island navigation within the same Balearic or Canary archipelago.

To cross from the mainland to the Balearics with the PER you need the relevant extension. Holding the licence doesn't replace practice: it includes manoeuvring and familiarisation training. Privileges are set out in Royal Decree 875/2014; it also limits what's authorised for the boat itself.

Which type of boat fits each use

  • Open or sundeck: day trips and swimming. The open frees up deck space; the sundeck prioritises the sunbathing area and often has a small cabin underneath.
  • Walkaround: lets you move easily around the console or cabin. Good for combining swimming, fishing and bow access; check the actual width of the side decks.
  • Cabin cruiser: shelter and overnight stays. In exchange, some of the space and budget goes to the interior.
  • Flybridge: a raised second helm station and another outdoor area. Gains social space, but adds height, stairs and surface exposed to wind.
  • RIB: practical for trips and swimming. The tubes take up space and need checking for material condition, valves, bonding and ageing.

The labels overlap: a walkaround can have a usable cabin. Compare layouts and use in the guide to types of motor boat, without ruling out models just because of how the seller markets them.

New or used: advantages and limits

New: you choose the configuration and know the history from day one. The outlay tends to be higher, and you need to pin down exactly what's included in the price: engine, electronics, transport and delivery. There can be lead times and teething issues; new doesn't mean problem-free.

Used: lets you get more length or equipment for the same budget. The trade-off is checking maintenance, corrosion, repairs and paperwork. Low hours alone aren't enough: an engine sitting idle for years can also need work.

I'd compare the cost of getting each candidate ready for your use. Check what to look at when buying a used boat, the ITB inspection history where relevant, and the availability of spare parts and service.

Typical first-time buyer mistakes

  • Buying without somewhere to keep it. Confirm the berth, its dimensions and price in writing.
  • Choosing on photos and extras alone. Try the layout and check that equipment actually works.
  • Confusing a discount with a saving. Add up pending repairs before negotiating.
  • Buying for an imagined holiday. Prioritise your actual regular outings.
  • Committing money without clear conditions. Put in writing what happens if documentation issues or survey findings come up.

Before deciding which boat to buy first, it can pay off to charter something similar or do an accompanied trial. Finding out the cabin is uncomfortable before you buy is cheaper than swapping boats afterwards.

Frequently asked questions

What's the best boat to start with?

Whatever you can handle, maintain and use often. For day trips, an open or a 5–6 metre RIB can fit the bill. If you need a heads or to sleep aboard, that changes the choice: there's no one-size-fits-all model.

Which boat should I buy first: new or used?

New lets you pick the configuration and know the full history from day one, at a higher cost. A used boat can offer more equipment for the same budget, but requires checking condition, paperwork and outstanding costs.

How much does it cost to maintain a 7–8 metre boat per year?

As a ballpark budget for 2026, expect €5,600–12,200 between mooring, insurance, routine maintenance and a contingency reserve in Catalonia. Fuel, financing, depreciation and holiday trips are calculated separately.

Can I sail from Barcelona to Ibiza with the PER licence?

The basic PER doesn't cover crossing between the mainland and the Balearics. You need the relevant extension or another licence with sufficient privileges, plus a suitable boat, equipment, experience and conditions.

Is it better to buy a longer boat so I don't have to upgrade later?

Only if you actually need that space and can afford it. More length usually means higher mooring costs, more maintenance and harder handling. Buying for a hypothetical use can leave you with an expensive boat you barely use.
