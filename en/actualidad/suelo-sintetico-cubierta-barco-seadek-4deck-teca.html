Synthetic decking: EVA (SeaDek, 4Deck) or synthetic teak, which to choose and what it costs

By Juan Morante11 September 20268 min read

This is one of the refits you see most in marinas right now: ripping out the old carpet or worn non-slip and fitting custom synthetic decking, almost always EVA foam with the seams marked to imitate teak. It changes the whole look of the boat, and the price it sells for along with it. But there are three different materials, prices nobody publishes, and details that make the difference between decking that lasts eight years and decking that peels off by the second summer. Here's what I look at.

The three materials you'll be offered

Closed-cell EVA foam (SeaDek, 4Deck, Ocevan and many white-label brands). A foam sheet 3 to 6 millimetres thick, sometimes thicker where people stand, cut to a template and bonded with 3M acrylic adhesive. It doesn't absorb water, it's soft underfoot, non-slip when wet, and heats up far less than teak in the sun, especially in light colours. It's the trendiest material on 5–12 metre motor boats because it's the cheapest of the three and the quickest to fit. Its limit is lifespan: manufacturers themselves talk about 5 to 8 years with good care, it scratches from sand and hooks, and dark colours fade.

PVC synthetic teak (Flexiteek, Permateek, Arteca, Nuteak). Strips of PVC welded together with a synthetic caulking seam, made up as a panel from a template of the boat and bonded with polyurethane adhesive. It looks like real teak until you touch it, lasts considerably longer than EVA, can be sanded if scratched and handles a pressure washer. In exchange it's heavier, costs more, heats up more in the sun than EVA, and installation is workshop work, not an afternoon job.

Cork (Marine Cork and similar). Less common on motor boats, natural, lightweight, a good thermal insulator and pleasant barefoot. It suits sailboats and classic boats; on an outboard boat that lives on coves and sand it's less practical.

Which I'd choose for each boat

An open, sundeck or RIB of 5–8 metres used in summer and stored on dry land: EVA, no question. The cost scales with the boat, it fits in a day, and you replace it when worn. A cabin cruiser or flybridge of 9 metres and up that you'll keep for years, or a boat you want to sell well: PVC synthetic teak on the cockpit and platforms, because at seven years it still looks the same and it shows in the valuation. And if you're unsure, a trick I use: EVA on the areas that get walked on and wet most (swim platform, cockpit) and nothing elsewhere; the boat gains a lot and the budget stays at half.

What it costs, and why nobody gives you a price per metre

No brand publishes rate cards, and it's not about hiding anything: every deck is a different pattern. The price comes from four things: the real square metres (not length times beam, but cockpit, platforms, side decks and hatches), the thickness and whether it's double-density, the number of pieces and cuts (a boat with lots of access hatches and steps costs more than one with two flat surfaces), and whether an old covering needs removing first, especially bonded real teak, which is charged separately. To give you a sense of scale, a 6-metre open has 3 to 5 square metres to cover; a 7.5-metre sundeck, 6 to 9; a 10-metre cabin cruiser with a flybridge, 12 to 18.

So when you ask for a quote, ask for it broken down like this: square metres measured off a template, material price per metre, labour, and removal of the old flooring if there is any. With that breakdown you can compare two offers; with a flat price and no square metres, you can't.

How it's installed properly (and how it's installed badly)

  • Template. The installer makes a physical template or a scan of each surface, and the decking is cut by numerical control. If they offer to "cut it by hand on the boat", the result shows at the edges and seams.
  • Prep. The surface has to be clean, dry and degreased, and the moulded non-slip texture in the gelcoat sanded down if it's very pronounced; otherwise the adhesive only grips the high points and lifts at the edges.
  • Temperature. EVA's acrylic adhesive needs pressure and a reasonable surface temperature; it doesn't bond well at eight in the morning in January, or on a deck baking in August.
  • Edges and hatches. Pieces should finish a couple of millimetres from the edges and respect access hatches so they still open; decking that covers a hatch is a problem the day you need to reach the tank.
  • Warranty. Ask what it covers and what it excludes: two years on material and manufacturing defects is typical; scratches, wear and errors in a design you approved aren't included.

Maintenance and what happens over the years

Fresh water, mild soap and a soft brush; no solvents and no pressure washer closer than half a metre on EVA (PVC can take it). EVA's enemy is sand underfoot and boat-hook hooks; PVC's is July heat, which makes dark decks impossible to walk on barefoot. If you store the boat outdoors in winter, a cover adds several years to either material.

What changes when you sell the boat

Well-fitted synthetic decking is one of the few upgrades you recover almost in full on sale, because it's the first thing seen in photos and the first thing stepped on at a viewing. Peeling or faded decking, on the other hand, subtracts more than it cost: the buyer assumes the rest of the boat is just as neglected. If you're selling within the next two years and the cockpit looks bad, it's an investment that usually pays off; if you're selling in three months, a deep clean and adjusting the price is the better move.
