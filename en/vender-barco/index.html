How I work

How it works

You can list it yourself, of course. The difference lies in everything that happens between publishing the listing and getting paid: the price, the listing itself, enquiries, viewings, negotiation and paperwork. You start with the form below or on WhatsApp, and this is what I do at each stage.

  1. 1

    Assessment and pricing

    Model, year, engine, hours, home port and the price you have in mind. I ask you about condition, pending work and paperwork, and check what comparable boats are asking and how long they've been listed, not just the headline price.

  2. 2

    Asking price in writing

    I propose an asking price and explain why, so you decide with real data. Then we sign the terms: the work, the fee and the length of the engagement, before we start.

  3. 3

    Listing, enquiries and viewings

    I gather maintenance records, equipment and paperwork, prepare the listing with your photos and publish it on the portals where buyers actually look. I answer every enquiry and speak with the interested party before the viewing: what they're after, their budget and when they want to buy. That way we can judge whether it's worth arranging the viewing.

  4. 4

    Offer, paperwork and handover

    I receive the offers, tell you what I think of each one, and negotiate on what we've agreed; the decision to accept is always yours. Then we coordinate the paperwork and handover. If you need a paperwork agency, survey or boatyard, we agree the scope and cost beforehand. And throughout, you know what enquiries are coming in and what keeps coming up.

Frequently asked questions

Do I have to remove my listings?

No. I work without exclusivity, so you can keep yours. Usually we align price and description so every listing says the same.

How much do you charge?

I set out my fees in writing in the brokerage agreement, fixed before we start. They are only charged if the boat is sold to a buyer I introduced: no advance payments or fixed costs. If an outside service were needed (paperwork agency, survey, boatyard, transport), whoever hires it pays for it, and we would agree its scope and cost beforehand.

How long will it take to sell?

It depends on the boat, the price and the time of year. With the valuation I give you a realistic guide, no promises.

Who receives the purchase price?

You do. The buyer pays directly into your account. I only invoice my fee.

Do you need to see the boat to get started?

No. We start with your photos, the documents and a conversation. When there is an interested buyer we arrange the viewing: depending on the boat and the area, I come along or we coordinate it with you.

What paperwork do I need to sell my boat?

Mainly the registration certificate, the navigation permit, a valid ITB inspection and an up-to-date registry extract with no charges, plus the sale contract. The full list is in the guide on paperwork for selling a boat. See the guide.

What taxes does the seller of a boat pay in Spain?

In a private sale, the buyer pays the transfer tax (ITP). You'd only need to declare it in your income tax return if you sell the boat for more than it cost you, because that difference is a capital gain. If you sell it for less, which is usual with used boats, there's nothing to pay, although that loss can't be offset either.

What happens to my berth if I sell the boat?

It depends on whether it's rented or owned. If it's yours and can be transferred with the sale, it's worth mentioning in the listing because it adds a lot. We'll look at it together when preparing the listing.

Can I sell a boat with a foreign flag?

Yes. What matters is explaining clearly to the buyer what the flag involves and what steps they'd need if they want to switch it to the Spanish flag. See the Polish flag guide.

Wondering what warranty you have to give when selling? I explain it in the guide on warranty in private boat sales.

Before listing it

What to have ready before selling your boat

You don't need everything on day one, but the sooner we gather it, the fewer surprises there will be when a buyer appears.

Up-to-date paperwork

Registration certificate, navigation permit, valid ITB inspection and a registry extract (hoja de asiento) with no charges. The full list is in the guide on paperwork for selling a boat.

Maintenance and invoices

Boatyard, engine and equipment invoices explain the boat's condition better than any description, and they give buyers confidence.

Good photos

They're the first thing a buyer looks at. I've put together a photo guide by boat type so you know which photos to take and how.

The berth

Whether it's owned or rented and, if it's yours, whether it could be transferred with the sale. For many buyers it's decisive.

Flag and origin

If the boat has a foreign flag or comes from List 6, it's best to know from the start. I explain it in the guides on the Polish flag and List 6 and List 7.

Price and timing

What affects the price and how long it takes to sell

The market sets the price: what similar boats are listed for, how long they've been on sale and what condition they're in. That's why, before suggesting an asking price, I look at real comparables and explain my reasoning.

What matters most is overall condition, the engine and its hours, maintenance you can prove, paperwork in order, the area and whether there's a berth. Timing counts too: there are more buyers in spring than at the end of the season, when sales tend to take longer.

You can get a first idea with the tool what affects your boat's value and the used boat prices by model. As for timing, I'd rather not promise dates: every boat is different and I'll give you a realistic idea once I know yours.

Worth keeping in mind

Common mistakes when selling a boat

Starting with a price that's too high

The boat goes weeks without enquiries and, by the time it's reduced, the listing already looks stale. A well-reasoned asking price works better.

Dark or cluttered photos

A good boat with poor photos looks worse than it is. Clean photos in natural light make a big difference to enquiries.

Finding a paperwork problem at the end

A charge on the registry or an expired ITB can stall the sale right at signing. Better to check beforehand.

Listings that contradict each other

Different prices or details on each portal create distrust. Everything should say the same.

Signing without a proper contract

A contract with the inventory, the declared condition and any charges in writing protects both sides.
