How to lay up the boat at the end of the season (and why it shows in the price)

By Juan MoranteSeptember 20266 min read

Come autumn, plenty of boats get ruined without ever leaving the mooring. What's done in these few weeks decides how it starts up in spring and, if the boat is for sale, what impression it gives at a viewing. This is the list I follow with my own.

Engine

It's the first thing, and the most expensive if neglected. On a petrol outboard or inboard: oil and filter change with the engine warm, lower-unit oil (if it comes out milky, there's water inside and seals need checking before storage), anodes, and a flush of the cooling circuit with fresh water. Also check the water pump impeller (both outboards and inboards have one) and the separator filter. If the boat won't move again until spring, fill the tank almost full so it doesn't condense, add fuel stabiliser and run the engine for a few minutes, with the boat in the water or with flush muffs on a hose, so it reaches the injection system.

An engine with maintenance done and logged in a notebook or on invoices is one of the cheapest selling points there is. When I value a boat, a clear maintenance history counts for more than most extras.

Hull and underwater surfaces

If the boat comes out of the water, it's the moment to check the underwater hull unhurried: osmosis blisters, antifouling condition, anodes on the leg and shafts, and the propeller (nicks, play). A pressure wash right after hauling out saves hours of scraping. If it stays in the water, at least check the anodes and clean the waterline.

Water, batteries and damp

  • Empty and rinse the fresh water tank and the black water tank; leave the taps open.
  • Batteries: if the boat stays in the water, keep the automatic bilge pump powered (charged battery or on a maintainer) and disconnect the rest. A battery that spends winter flat often doesn't make it to spring.
  • Ventilation: a dehumidifier if there's power, or at least open lockers, cushions on edge and a couple of moisture absorbers. The smell of a closed-up boat puts off plenty of buyers.
  • Fridge clean, empty and with the door left slightly open.

Deck, covers and lines

Wash the whole deck, fittings and windows with fresh water; salt leaves marks that are a pain to remove later. Remove or loosen any cover that could pool water, and store cushions and the bimini somewhere dry if you can. Check mooring lines and fenders: this is when there's most wind and most days go by without anyone looking at the boat. A worn line in November can end up as a boat against the dock in January.

Paperwork and insurance

Use this time to check dates: ITB inspection expiry, liferaft service if fitted, flares, extinguishers and the insurance receipt. If you're thinking of selling in spring, having all this in order by March saves you weeks: buyers ask for it, and paperwork agents get busy in season.

If the boat is for sale

A boat for sale in winter needs to be ready to show in twenty minutes. That means a clean deck, a dry and aired interior, an engine that starts first time, and documents in a folder. Nothing more is needed, but that much has to be there. If it's stored ashore, sort out access with the boatyard before the first interested buyer shows up, because that's what loses the most viewings.
