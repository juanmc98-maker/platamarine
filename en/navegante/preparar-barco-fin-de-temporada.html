Laying up a boat for winter: how I get mine ready

By Juan MoranteChecked on 3 November 202612 min read

Come autumn, plenty of boats get ruined without ever leaving the mooring. What's done in these few weeks decides how it starts up in spring and, if the boat is for sale, what impression it gives at a viewing. This is the list I follow with my own, written for the Mediterranean: frost is rare here, but damp and winter storms are not.

The short answer: laying up in 5 steps

If you only have five minutes, this is what I never skip. The detail is further down and, at the end, there's a checklist to tick off.

  1. Engine. Oil and filters changed, raw-water circuit flushed with fresh water and the fuel ready to sit, always following your engine manual.
  2. Water out. Tanks, water heater, heads and bilge empty, clean and dry.
  3. Batteries charged. Switch everything off except the automatic bilge pump if the boat stays afloat.
  4. Hull and mooring. Anodes, seacocks, mooring lines and fenders checked. Cover well secured and ventilated.
  5. Dry interior. Ventilation or a dehumidifier, gas turned off and the dates of the safety gear written down.

Afloat or ashore?

In the Mediterranean plenty of boats spend the winter afloat without any trouble, as long as someone drops by to check on them. On the Catalan coast, right by the sea, frost is rare. What you do get is damp, rain and easterly storms that put mooring lines to the test.

  • Afloat. Easier and usually cheaper. The sea acts as a thermal buffer and the engine is still there to run now and then. In return, you need the bilge pump powered, mooring lines in good condition and a visit after every storm.
  • Ashore. No risk of sinking or chafing against the pontoon, and it's a chance to check and paint the underwater hull. In return, every water circuit has to be drained properly, the boat well chocked, and you pay for the lift and the stay.

If your marina can freeze

A reservoir, an inland harbour or a boat stored ashore away from the coast can see nights below zero. There, any circuit holding water that can't be fully drained needs antifreeze, of the type your manual specifies. On a seafront berth draining and flushing is usually enough, but check the forecast and don't take it for granted.

Engine

It comes first, and it's the most expensive thing to neglect. Every engine has its own lay-up instructions and they override anything you read here. What follows is the order I work in.

Inboard diesel

  • Change the oil and filter with the engine warm. Used oil carries acids you don't want sitting inside all winter.
  • Fuel filters and water separator bowl: drain off the water and change the filters if they're due.
  • Tank nearly full, so that water doesn't condense inside. If you've had diesel bug before, ask about a biocide and use it as the maker says.
  • Raw-water circuit: flush it with fresh water. If the boat may freeze, the manual will tell you whether to run antifreeze through and which one.
  • Closed circuit: check the coolant level and condition. If it's due by hours or by years, better now than in May.
  • Water pump impeller: check it. Some manufacturers recommend taking it out over winter so it doesn't take a set; if you do, leave a clearly visible note.
  • Belts, hoses and clamps: a quick look for cracks or rust.
  • If it has a saildrive or sterndrive, change the oil and check it doesn't come out milky.
  • Plug the air intake and the exhaust outlet to keep damp out, and leave a note on the ignition so you remember to remove them.

An engine with its maintenance done and written down in a log or backed by invoices is one of the cheapest selling points there is. When I value a boat, a clear maintenance history counts for more than most extras.

Petrol inboard and sterndrive

  • Oil and filter with the engine warm, just like a diesel.
  • Petrol goes stale quickly when it sits. Add stabiliser and run the engine for a few minutes so it reaches the whole system, as the maker says.
  • Some manuals call for fogging oil in the cylinders, mainly on carburettor engines. Fuel-injected engines aren't always done that way: follow yours.
  • Sterndrive oil: if it comes out milky, there's water inside and the seals need checking before lay-up.
  • Sterndrive bellows: check them for cracks. A split bellows can let water into the boat.
  • If the boat may freeze, the block and manifolds are drained through their plugs or protected with antifreeze, as per the manual. A block cracked by frost is one of the most expensive failures there is.
  • In spring, ventilate the engine bay before starting. With petrol, always.

Outboard

  • Flush it with fresh water using muffs or the flushing port many models have. Never run it without water: the impeller burns out in seconds.
  • Fuel: stabiliser and a few minutes running so it reaches the injection or the carburettor. Some makers ask you to drain the carburettor float bowl; check yours.
  • Engine oil (on four-strokes) and gear oil. If the gear oil is milky, take it to the workshop.
  • Grease the points the manual shows, check the anodes and take a look at the propeller.
  • If you take it off, store it upright so all the water drains out. If it has to lie down, put it on the side the manufacturer specifies; on the other side oil can end up where it shouldn't.
  • Portable tank: closed, somewhere ventilated and well away from any flame.

Fresh water, heads and bilge

  • Empty the fresh-water tank and open the taps until the pump runs dry. Switch the pump off and leave the taps open.
  • Drain the water heater too. It's the first thing to split if it freezes.
  • If your marina can freeze and you can't drain everything, use only antifreeze that is safe for drinking-water systems. Car antifreeze is toxic: not in the water circuit and not in the heads.
  • Heads: pump out the holding tank at the marina's pump-out station, rinse with fresh water and use the product the toilet maker recommends.
  • Bilge clean and dry. Test the bilge pump by lifting the float by hand. If there's any oil, soak it up with absorbent pads: oily water is never pumped into the sea.

Batteries

A battery that spends the winter flat often doesn't make it to spring. And a flat lead-acid battery can also freeze; a charged one is much harder to freeze.

  • Flooded lead-acid. Charge them fully, clean the terminals and check the electrolyte level if you can.
  • AGM and gel. Also fully charged, with a charger that has the right profile. The wrong profile damages them little by little.
  • Lithium (LiFePO4). Store them at the state of charge the maker recommends, which is often not 100 %. Don't charge them below 0 °C unless they have built-in heating. The battery management system (BMS) usually cuts charging in the cold, but it's best not to rely on that alone.
  • Turn the main switch off so nothing drains them. If the boat stays afloat, keep the automatic bilge pump powered.
  • If there's shore power, a maintainer with the right profile keeps them topped up all winter. If not, check them every few weeks or take them home.

Hull, anodes, seacocks, mooring lines and covers

Hull and underwater surfaces

If the boat comes out of the water, this is the moment to look over the underwater hull calmly: osmosis blisters, the state of the antifouling, shafts, drive and propeller (dings, play). A pressure wash straight after lifting saves hours of scraping. If it stays afloat, at least clean the waterline.

Anodes

Check them all: hull, shafts, drive or saildrive and, if fitted, the bow thruster. If they're badly worn, replace them before you leave the boat. A spent anode protects nothing, and winter is long.

Seacocks

Afloat, open and close them a couple of times so they don't seize, and check hoses and clamps. Close the ones you don't need, but never the cockpit drains: they have to be able to clear rainwater. Ashore, leave them open so they drain, and grease them if the model calls for it.

Mooring lines, fenders and covers

Wash the whole deck, fittings and windows with fresh water; salt leaves marks that are hard to remove later. Double up the lines that work hardest, fit chafe protection where they rub and check the fenders: this is when there's most wind and the most days go by without anyone looking at the boat. A worn line in November can end with a boat against the pontoon in January.

The cover should be taut so it doesn't collect pools of water, and ventilated. An airtight cover traps the damp, and in spring you find mould. Store cushions and the bimini somewhere dry if you can.

Sails, rigging and winches (sailing boats)

  • Take the genoa off the furler. A furled sail can unroll in a storm and tear, battering your boat and the one next to it.
  • Wash the sails with fresh water, dry them completely and store them folded somewhere dry. A sail stowed damp ends up mouldy.
  • The mainsail, either taken off or well protected by its cover.
  • Halyards can be swapped for thin messenger lines so they're out of the sun and don't slap the mast.
  • Standing rigging: check terminals, spreaders and turnbuckles for cracks or rust. If in doubt, have a rigger look at it.
  • Winches: strip, clean and grease them as the maker specifies. Pawls usually take oil rather than grease; the winch manual makes it clear.

Interior: damp, gas, electronics and safety gear

  • Ventilation: a dehumidifier if there's power and the marina allows it, or at least lockers open, cushions on edge and a couple of moisture absorbers. The smell of a shut-up boat puts a lot of buyers off.
  • Fridge clean, empty and with the door ajar.
  • No food or rubbish aboard.
  • Gas: close the bottle, disconnect it and check the expiry date on the flexible hose.
  • Electronics: portable kit (handheld VHF, GPS) goes home. Fixed screens get their covers on.

Safety gear and paperwork

It's a good time to note down dates: flares and other pyrotechnics, extinguishers, liferaft, inflatable lifejackets and the first-aid kit. In Spain, Royal Decree 339/2021 refers extinguisher servicing to the fire-protection regulations, and liferaft and inflatable lifejacket servicing to the manufacturer's instructions.

Check the expiry date of the survey (ITB) and the insurance renewal too. If you're thinking of selling in spring, having all this in order by March saves you weeks: buyers ask for it, and the agencies that handle paperwork are swamped in season.

Costly mistakes

  • Car antifreeze in the drinking-water system or the heads. It's toxic. Only drinking-water-safe antifreeze, and only if needed.
  • Running the outboard dry "just for a second". The impeller burns out in seconds.
  • An airtight cover with no ventilation. What's waiting for you in spring is mould.
  • Batteries left connected to something that draws a little. Over three months, that little drains them.
  • Leaving the genoa furled through the winter storms.
  • Forgetting the intake and exhaust plugs when starting in spring. That's what the note on the ignition is for.
  • Cockpit drains blocked by the cover or full of leaves. Rainwater has to be able to get out.
  • A half-empty diesel tank. The air inside condenses into water.

If the boat is for sale

A boat for sale in winter needs to be ready to show in twenty minutes. That means a clean deck, a dry and aired interior, an engine that starts first time, and documents in a folder. Nothing more is needed, but that much has to be there. If it's stored ashore, sort out access with the boatyard before the first interested buyer shows up, because that's what loses the most viewings.
