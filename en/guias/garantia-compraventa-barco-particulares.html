Warranty when buying or selling a boat between private parties in Spain: what the law says

By Juan MoranteUpdated September 20269 min read

Between private parties there is no commercial warranty like a dealer's, but the seller is liable for hidden defects the boat already had at delivery, even if they didn't know about them. For leisure boats, the deadlines are set by Spain's Maritime Navigation Act and they are very short: 3 months to discover the defect, 5 days to notify it and 6 months to claim.

Which law applies: maritime law, not just the Civil Code

The sale of vessels is regulated by Law 14/2014 on Maritime Navigation (LNM), articles 117 to 121. Article 121 extends those rules to leisure craft "insofar as their nature allows". Article 2.1 provides that, where the LNM is silent, general civil law applies.

In practice: the deadlines are those of the LNM, and the Civil Code fills in the rest (what a hidden defect is, what can be claimed, what can be agreed). The general six-month period in article 1490 of the Civil Code is not the one that counts for these boats. The Provincial Court of Alicante applied it this way in judgment 72/2019, for example.

One nuance: the LNM covers maritime navigation. A boat used only on inland waters (a reservoir, for example) may fall outside it, and not every court cites the LNM in every case. That is why the safe approach is always to meet the short deadlines.

What a private seller is liable for

The seller must deliver what was agreed (the boat, its inventory and the agreed documents), guarantee that nobody will take the boat from the buyer on the basis of an earlier right (eviction), and answer for undisclosed charges and hidden defects.

For a defect to be a hidden defect it must be (art. 1484 Civil Code):

  • Pre-existing: it existed, at least in its origin, when the boat was delivered. A new breakdown does not count.
  • Serious: it makes the boat unfit for use, or reduces its usefulness so much that the buyer would not have bought it or would have paid less.
  • Hidden: it was not visible or easy to detect. A defect disclosed in writing stops being hidden to the extent it was disclosed.

Normal wear from age and hours is not a hidden defect. Serious hidden deterioration, inconsistent with the condition described, can be. Paperwork counts too: in the Alicante case, false CE documents that prevented the boat from being registered were treated as a hidden defect even though the seller acted in good faith.

Deadlines to claim: 3 months, 5 days and 6 months

The three deadlines are linked. Miss one and the hidden-defect claim is lost, even if the defect is real.

  • 3 months from physical delivery to discover the defect. Delivery counts, not signing: the buyer becomes owner on delivery (art. 118.2 LNM). That is why the delivery date should be recorded in writing.
  • 5 days from discovery to notify the seller in a verifiable way. Not from the end of the three months.
  • 6 months from that notice to bring the claim. It is a strict time bar.

How to notify the defect so that it counts

"Verifiable" (fehaciente) means you can prove the content, the date and receipt. The safest option is a burofax with certified content or a notarial record. An email or letter with proof of receipt and of its content, or a document signed as received by the seller, can also work.

Notice goes to the seller, directly. Telling only the broker is not enough, unless the seller expressly authorised the broker to receive such notices or it can be proved that the notice reached the seller in time.

Does a WhatsApp message count?

In judgment 963/2019 of the Las Palmas court, the buyer of a sailing yacht found osmosis at the end of June and warned by phone and WhatsApp from 10 July. He lost because no notice of any kind arrived within the five days. The court did not say WhatsApp is invalid: the problem was the deadline. Even so, for something this important, use a method that leaves no doubt.

How it is proved and what can be claimed

The burden of proof is on the claimant: the buyer must show the defect exists, was hidden, already existed at delivery and is serious (art. 217 Civil Procedure Act). A repair quote is not enough: a technical report explaining the cause, how long it has existed and its extent is needed. Before repairing, let the seller inspect it unless it is urgent.

The buyer can choose (art. 1486 Civil Code):

  • Undo the sale: refund of the price and the contract costs.
  • A proportional price reduction.
  • Damages on top if the seller knew of the defect and did not disclose it, and the buyer chooses to undo the sale.

If the seller did not know of the defect, they answer for the price and contract costs, but not for other losses: in Barcelona judgment 406/2020 the seller did not have to pay for upgrades ordered by the buyers or for storage. And a price reduction is not compensation: the Supreme Court (judgment 865/2003) did not allow reducing the price by the repair cost and also recovering that same repair.

Can a boat be sold "as is"?

Yes, within limits. Private parties can agree in writing that the seller will not answer for hidden defects they are unaware of (arts. 1255 and 1485 Civil Code). But:

  • It must be clear. The Madrid court (judgment 6/2026) required an agreement that is "conclusive and categorical": it was not enough for the buyer to declare the boat "in perfect condition" and take on the responsibilities of ownership.
  • It does not cover what the seller knew and kept quiet about.
  • It does not cover fraud, which cannot be waived in advance (art. 1102 Civil Code).
  • It does not cover express promises, such as an agreed repair.

Whether the 3-month and 5-day deadlines can also be changed by contract has not been confirmed by any judgment we have found. The safe choice is not to touch them.

Mandatory negotiation before going to court (LO 1/2025)

Since April 2025, you must try to negotiate before suing. Direct negotiation between the parties or their lawyers counts, as well as mediation or conciliation (art. 5 LO 1/2025).

  • It suspends the 6-month period from the documented attempt to deliver the invitation to negotiate, provided it clearly states what is to be negotiated (art. 7.1).
  • The period resumes with the time that was left, it does not restart: if there is no meeting or written reply within 30 calendar days, if a specific proposal gets no answer within 30 days, 3 months after the first meeting without agreement, or when one party ends the negotiation in writing (arts. 7 and 10).
  • Do not rely on it for the 3 months and the 5 days: those deadlines must be met anyway.
  • The one year in art. 7.3 is not an extra year to claim: it lets the negotiation count as the pre-trial requirement, but the claim is still subject to whatever remains of the 6 months.
  • What is negotiated is confidential (art. 9). That is why the defect notice should be sent separately from the invitation to negotiate.

If the boat is different from what was agreed

When what is delivered is something else, or is entirely unfit for its purpose, Spanish law speaks of aliud pro alio. The buyer can then seek termination of the contract for breach (arts. 1101 and 1124 Civil Code), a different claim from the hidden-defect warranty, with a five-year limitation period (art. 1964.2 Civil Code). Courts reserve it for serious cases where the purchase is completely frustrated; it cannot be used to revive an ordinary defect claim whose deadline has passed.

What about the broker?

The broker is not the seller: the sale is signed between seller and buyer, and its effects arise between them (arts. 1257 and 1725 Civil Code). At Plata Marine:

  • We tell you from the start who the private seller is.
  • The price is paid directly into the seller's bank account. Plata Marine only invoices its brokerage fee.
  • We give no warranty of our own on the boat's condition and always recommend a survey and sea trial.

Like any professional, a broker is liable for their own conduct: for example, if they stated technical facts they could not back up.

Checklist before buying a used boat

  1. Independent survey, out of the water (hull, osmosis, shafts, propellers, rudder) and afloat.
  2. Sea trial with the engines under load.
  3. Movable Property Registry report (mortgages, seizures, retention of title) and a check with the Harbour Master's office.
  4. Annex of known defects, signed by both parties.
  5. Written contract, with the date and place of physical delivery and addresses for notices.

If a fault appears afterwards: document it, notify the seller within five days by a method that leaves a record, let them inspect it before repairing and, if there is no agreement, claim within the six months.

Frequently asked questions

How long do I have to claim for a defect in a boat bought from a private seller?

The defect must be discovered within 3 months of physical delivery, notified to the seller within 5 days of discovery, and the claim brought within 6 months of that notice (arts. 119.2 and 120 of the Maritime Navigation Act).

Do I have to send a burofax?

Not necessarily, but the notice must be able to prove content, date and receipt. A burofax with certified content is the safest option.

Is telling the broker instead of the seller enough?

Only if the seller expressly authorised the broker to receive these notices, or if it can be proved that the notice reached the seller in time. It is best always to notify the seller directly.

Can a boat be sold without warranty?

The parties can agree that the seller will not answer for defects they are unaware of, with a clear clause. It never covers what the seller knew and concealed, or fraud.

What if the seller knew about the defect?

Besides undoing the sale and recovering the price, the buyer can claim damages. A warranty waiver does not protect the seller.
