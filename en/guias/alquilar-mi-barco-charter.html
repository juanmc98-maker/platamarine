Can I charter out my boat to cover the costs?

By Juan MoranteUpdated October 20268 min read

It’s one of the questions owners ask me most: “what if I rent it out for a few weeks in summer to cover the berth?”. It can be done, but not the way many people imagine. Here is what Spanish regulations say as of October 2026, with sources, so you can decide with the numbers in front of you.

The short answer

A private owner can’t simply rent out their boat “now and then” and get paid. Renting out a recreational boat is a business activity: you have to be registered, meet stricter safety and insurance requirements and declare the income. There are two routes, and neither lets you keep using the boat as usual while it’s being rented.

The two legal routes

1. Move the boat to list 6 (commercial use). This is the route charter companies take: the boat is dedicated to rental, passes commercial vessel inspections and carries the corresponding insurance. I explain the differences between lists in the guide to list 6 and list 7.

2. The temporary switch to commercial use, staying on list 7. Since 31 December 2025 it has been governed by Royal Decree 1188/2025, which amended article 9 of the Maritime Navigation Regulation (RD 186/2023). The conditions:

  • A maximum of 3 consecutive months per calendar year.
  • The boat must be managed by a charter company under a management contract, and it is that company that applies for authorisation.
  • During that period, neither the owner nor related persons may use it or work on board (except professional crew permanently signed on).
  • It follows the list 6 inspection regime, carries “CT” before the registration number on both bows and needs accident insurance for everyone on board.

Requirements that always apply

  • Maximum 12 passengers plus crew. Above that, passenger ship rules apply.
  • Bareboat: the charterer is responsible for whoever skippers holding the required licence.
  • Skippered: the skipper and crew need professional qualifications; a recreational licence is not enough.
  • Clearance: responsible declaration, crew list, valid policies and being up to date with taxes.
  • Third-party liability insurance with the minimums of RD 607/1999 (€120,202.42 per victim and €240,404.84 per incident for personal injury; €96,161.94 for property damage) and passenger accident insurance (RD 1575/1989).
  • Registration under IAE heading 855.2 (“Boat rental”), even for a single boat, according to binding tax ruling V0249-05. For a private individual, that means doing it as self-employed (autónomo).

Balearic Islands: chartering on list 7 is banned

Balearic Decree 44/2025 (BOIB, 2 August 2025) requires list 6 for charter and bans the temporary switch to commercial use, as well as selling individual seats. It also requires a responsible declaration, entry in the Balearic register, a fee, proof of berth or port authorisation, insurance, a contract showing the declaration number, a passenger log and a numbered flag on board.

The decree has been challenged, but the Balearic High Court refused to suspend it on 8 January 2026, so it remains in force. And it is enforced: in 2025 the regional government opened 162 cases for illegal charter, with €471,600 in proposed fines. Remember too that anchoring on posidonia seagrass is prohibited (Decree 25/2018).

Catalonia

I haven’t found a specific Catalan regulation like the Balearic one. Until one is published, national rules apply: list 6 or the temporary switch from list 7 with a management company and a maximum of 3 months. Before taking any step, check with the Harbour Master’s Office (Capitanía Marítima) in your area and with your port, because not every berth allows commercial activity.

Tax: what most people overlook

  • VAT: charter is subject to 21%.
  • Registration tax (IEDMT): boats used effectively and exclusively for charter can be exempt, with prior recognition by the tax office (art. 66 of Law 38/1992). If the owner or a related person uses it privately, it no longer counts as rental and the exemption is lost (ruling V1307-2025). I explain it in the guide to the registration tax.
  • VAT on the purchase: only deductible if the business activity is genuine from the start and can be proven. For mixed use (a few weeks chartered, the rest for you) there is no clear criterion: check with your adviser before buying.
  • Income tax: what you earn is business income and must be declared as such.

What nobody tells you

Advertised earnings are usually gross. Before doing the maths, ask about everything that eats into the margin: the management company’s share, pricier insurance, extra inspections, wear on engines and interiors, cleaning between clients and a season that in practice is concentrated into a few weeks. I don’t give figures because they vary a lot by boat, area and company: ask for them in writing.

So, is it worth it?

It depends on what you want. If you buy a boat to run it as a business, with the numbers done and a serious management company, it can make sense. If what you want is to sail yourself and have charter “pay for the berth”, it clashes with the law: during the charter months you can’t use it, and in the Balearics you can’t do it on list 7. Sometimes the sensible thing is to choose a boat whose costs you can cover on your own; for that you have the boat running cost calculator.

General information based on RD 186/2023 (amended by RD 1188/2025), RD 607/1999, RD 1575/1989, Law 38/1992, Balearic Decree 44/2025 and rulings of the Spanish Directorate General of Taxes, reviewed in October 2026. This is not legal or tax advice and regulations may change. Check with the Harbour Master’s Office, your gestoría or a lawyer before chartering.
