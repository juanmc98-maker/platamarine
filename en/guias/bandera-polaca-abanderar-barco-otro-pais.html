Polish flag, Gibraltar, Malta and other foreign flags: what you really save, and what you don't

By Juan MoranteSeptember 202612 min read

Every week I see listings for boats "with Polish flag, no registration tax", and buyers asking whether it's worth it for them. The short answer: for someone who lives in Spain and uses the boat here, the flag changes less than it seems, and it usually counts against you on resale. Here's what I've understood of the rules, leaving the sales pitch aside.

What flagging abroad actually means

Flagging a boat means registering it in a country's registry. That country gives it its nationality, its registration document and its inspection rules. A resident of Spain can register their boat in the Spanish registry (list 7, with a registration number, ITB and so on) or, in some countries, in a foreign registry that accepts non-residents. Poland has been the most popular choice for years because it's cheap, fast and doesn't require residency.

What often gets overlooked is that the flag only changes the obligations set by the country of registration. The obligations Spain imposes on its residents for using a boat in Spanish waters stay exactly the same, whatever flag it flies.

The Polish flag, as it stands today

Since August 2020 the Polish registry for boats up to 24 metres has been a single electronic system (REJA24), run by local authorities and by the Polish sailing and powerboating federations. Registration is compulsory for boats with a Polish connection and voluntary for any other owner who requests it, with no nationality or residency requirement. Boats owned by residents of Spain come in through that voluntary route. The Polish government itself estimates that around 36,000 boats on its registry belong to foreigners.

What's needed: proof of ownership (invoice or contract), the boat's technical details, the CE declaration if there is one, ID for the owner or a company extract if it's owned by a company, and a sworn Polish translation of any documents not already in Polish. Right now there's no prior inspection or compulsory insurance to register a leisure boat. The official fee is 80 zloty, about 19 euros; the rest of what agencies charge (roughly 440–470 euros) is handling and translation. The certificate doesn't expire and carries no annual fee.

Watch out for 2027

On 28 July 2026 the Polish government approved a bill to change this: prior inspection by an authorised organisation for boats seeking the Polish flag, confirmation of register data at least every two years (otherwise automatic removal) and no more registration for boats under 7.5 metres or with engines of up to 15 kW. Much higher fees have also been discussed, but the final amount is yet to be seen. The bill has been before the Polish parliament since August 2026 and hasn't been voted on, so it may change; even so, it's worth knowing that the "cheap and forever" flag is probably on borrowed time. I go into detail in this article on the reform.

What stays the same for a resident of Spain

Registration tax

This is the point that causes the most confusion. The tax (IEDMT) isn't paid for registering in Spain: it's paid for using a boat over 8 metres in Spain as a resident. The Excise Tax Law states that boats intended for use in Spanish territory by residents must be registered here, and that if registration hasn't been requested within 30 days of that use starting, the tax falls due anyway. So a resident with a 9-metre boat under a Polish flag moored in Blanes, in principle, owes the 12% just as if it had been registered in Spain. The Directorate-General for Taxation has restated this in recent rulings (for example, V0820-25), and, according to specialist firms, in practice the Guardia Civil and Customs Surveillance can impound the boat until it's paid or guaranteed. I cover this in more detail in the registration tax guide.

Put the other way round: a foreign flag only saves that tax for someone who isn't a resident of Spain, or who uses the boat outside Spain. For everyone else, it's most likely a tax bill waiting to happen.

Licence

The Royal Decree on boating licences states that whoever skippers a foreign-flagged boat in Spanish waters needs the licence of the flag country if they hold that nationality, and if not, the licence of the country where they reside. A resident of Spain with a Polish boat needs, as the rule is worded, the PER, the Yacht Skipper licence or whichever applies by length, exactly as with a Spanish flag.

Insurance and safety equipment

Compulsory third-party liability insurance applies to any boat sailing in Spanish waters, and the Spanish safety-equipment rules apply expressly to boats of any flag whose owner is resident in Spain. The Spanish ITB inspection, on the other hand, doesn't apply to a Polish-flagged boat; Poland currently has no periodic inspection requirement for leisure boats, and that's the most tangible saving: the five-yearly inspection and its cost.

Taxes when buying second-hand

If you buy from a private individual as a resident of Spain, transfer tax is due regardless of the flag (5% in Catalonia, within the following 30 working days). And VAT: always ask for proof that VAT has been paid on the boat within the European Union (original invoice or customs document). A foreign-flagged boat without proven VAT status is a problem that can end up landing on the buyer.

What happens when you sell

This is where it shows most. A Spanish buyer of a Polish-flagged boat over 8 metres has to register it in Spain and pay the 12% registration tax on its value, plus paperwork, translations and the initial ITB. Many buyers know this, or their accountant tells them, and they usually deduct it from the price or look at a different boat. From what I see, foreign-flagged boats take longer to sell and get negotiated down further; Spanish insurers and lenders are also more reluctant. When I value a foreign-flagged boat I factor this in, and when I'm advising a buyer, the first thing I ask for is the VAT status and a calculation of what it would cost to flag it in Spain.

Other flags you'll see in listings

  • Netherlands (ICP). Not a flag: it's an international boat identification certificate, and for years it's only been issued to Dutch nationals or residents of the Netherlands for more than five years. A boat "with an ICP" has no registry.
  • Belgium. Since September 2019 it requires a Belgian connection (a Belgian owner or resident, or a Belgian company). In practice, hard for a private individual from here.
  • United Kingdom (SSR). Requires UK residency and, after Brexit, the boat counts as coming from outside the EU: temporary importation and VAT status come into play. Not really advisable for someone sailing in Spain.
  • Delaware. A state registry for use in Delaware; it doesn't confer US nationality for international purposes, it isn't recognised as a flag, and some European harbours have turned away boats with this registration. It can cause problems with the authorities.
  • Portugal (Madeira registry). An EU flag for boats of 7 metres or more, with inspection by a classification society and around 500 euros to register plus 500 a year.
  • San Marino. Not in the EU. Open to any nationality through an agent, roughly 500 to 1,900 euros depending on length and duration, plus the agent's fee; doesn't confer VAT-paid status.

Yachts: Gibraltar and Malta

On larger boats, 15 metres and up, the flags you see most aren't Polish but Gibraltar and Malta, and here the issue isn't just registration tax: it's VAT.

Gibraltar

The Gibraltar registry accepts EU citizens in their own name (the 2021 reform, after Brexit, kept that option and extended it to the Commonwealth and about twenty other countries), with a representative in Gibraltar if you don't live there, and companies too; the small registry without a title of ownership is only for Gibraltar residents. It requires tonnage measurement under 24 metres and a fuller international measurement above that (plus class if used commercially). Official fees are modest (around £254 to register and £29 a year under 24 metres, per the August 2026 tariff), though agents charge packages of roughly £1,150 to £3,000.

What matters: Gibraltar sits outside the EU's VAT territory, and the registry itself notes that Gibraltar has no VAT. That means a Gibraltar-flagged boat that has never been cleared within the EU is, for customs purposes, a boat from outside (the flag doesn't change the status: what counts is whether VAT was paid within the EU). A non-resident can keep it in European waters under temporary importation, VAT-free, for up to 18 months. A resident of Spain can't: to use it here they have to clear it for free circulation and pay VAT (21%) and duty, plus registration tax if it's over 8 metres. Many yachts advertised "Gibraltar-flagged, VAT unpaid" at an attractive price are exactly that: unpaid VAT waiting for a buyer living in Spain. And since 15 July 2026 the agreement between the EU and the UK on Gibraltar, under provisional application, changes the Rock's customs regime; specialist firms warn that using Gibraltar to "restart" the 18-month temporary importation clock may come to an end, though the notes published so far by Spain's tax agency on the agreement are general and say nothing specific about yachts or temporary importation, and during the transitional phase Gibraltar is still treated as a third country.

Malta

Malta is the largest registry in the EU and a serious flag: solid ship mortgages, a commercial yacht code for charter, tonnage tax for owning companies and electronic certificates. It can register an EU citizen in their own name or a Maltese company (those from outside the EU, through a company and a resident agent). A provisional six-month registration comes first, then the permanent one with the inspection and tonnage certificates; as a general rule, boats over 15 years old undergo a prior inspection and those over 25 only get in with the registry's authorisation. Official fees are low (under 24 metres, around 115 euros to register and 25 or 150 a year depending on tonnage; over 24 metres, based on net tonnage), but the resident agent and management typically add between roughly 750 and 3,500 euros a year depending on size and structure, which is what makes it not worth it on a 9-metre boat and worthwhile on a 20-metre one.

What makes Malta famous is its VAT regime for chartered yachts: since 2020, Maltese VAT (18%) applies only to the portion of the charter that corresponds to actual use in EU waters, evidenced by the logbook and AIS, and at the end of the contract the boat can end up with VAT-paid status. It's a structure for large boats with a tax adviser behind it, not for a private individual. And it doesn't touch Spanish registration tax: Spain's tax authority has told a Maltese company chartering a yacht out of Mallorca that the boat remains subject to the tax, and that the rental exemption only applies if the company registers for business activity tax (IAE), dedicates the boat exclusively to charter (not leasing it to related parties or to the same client for more than three months a year) and keeps those conditions for four years.

In short: for a resident of Spain, Malta makes sense if the boat is large, will be chartered, or the owner is a company with an adviser; Gibraltar is really only useful for non-residents. For a private individual with an 8 to 15-metre boat sailing in Catalonia, neither one saves you the registration tax, and Gibraltar can add VAT on top.

When it makes sense, and when it doesn't

It makes sense if you're not a resident of Spain and sail here seasonally, if you'll keep the boat outside Spain, or if the boat has no CE marking and flagging it in Spain is a technical problem. It's also used by companies and chartered boats with structures that need a specialist manager, which isn't my field.

It doesn't make sense if you live in Spain, use the boat in Spain, and what you're after is avoiding registration tax: you almost certainly won't avoid it, and on resale you pay for it twice, in the discount and in time on the market. If you're looking at a boat advertised with a Polish flag, my advice is to run the numbers with your accountant before making an offer: VAT status, the cost of registering in Spain and, if it's over 8 metres, the 12%.
