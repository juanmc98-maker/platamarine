List 6 and list 7: what they mean and what changes when you buy or sell

By Juan MoranteUpdated September 20267 min read

In any listing for a boat over 8 metres you'll run into these two terms: list 6 or list 7. It's not just an administrative detail. It changes the VAT, whether there's outstanding registration tax, how often the inspection is due, what insurance you need and, above all, whether the price you're seeing is the real price. This guide explains each list and what to check before buying an ex-charter boat.

What each list is

Spain's ship registry sorts boats into lists by use. For the used leisure-boat market, only two really matter:

  • List 7: private leisure use, not for profit. This is the normal list for a private boat: one you buy to sail yourself, not to rent out.
  • List 6: commercial use. Charter, sailing schools, fishing tourism. The boat is registered as a business activity and operated to generate income.

The same boat model, same year, same length, can be on one list or the other depending on what its owner has done with it. And that's exactly what's worth checking before buying, because the list's history sticks to the boat.

VAT and IEDMT: the difference that weighs most on price

On list 7, a new boat pays 21% VAT like any purchase. If it's over 8 metres, it also pays 12% IEDMT on first registration (exempt up to 8 m, with form 06). I cover this in more detail in the registration tax guide.

On list 6, a boat used exclusively and genuinely for charter can be registered exempt from IEDMT, recognised in advance by the tax authority using form 05. VAT can also be deductible where there's real business activity. In exchange, the boat has to meet every requirement for a commercial vessel: insurance, inspections and equipment at a different level.

Here's the point that really matters to a second-hand buyer: that IEDMT exemption on list 6 isn't free or permanent. It comes with a condition known as the 4-year rule, which I explain below because it's the one that causes the most surprises.

Inspections: the ITB isn't the same on both lists

The Boat Technical Inspection (ITB) is the compulsory periodic check, the equivalent of a car's MOT. On list 7, for boats of 6 to 15 metres, it's done every 5 years. On list 6 inspections are more frequent: every 3 years for boats of 6 metres or more, with intermediate or annual checks depending on the boat's equipment. A boat that's been on charter usually has a denser inspection history, which in principle is a good sign of maintenance, but it's worth checking against the actual certificates rather than assuming anything.

Insurance and equipment: a different level on list 6

A boat on list 6 needs commercial insurance, not the usual leisure policy, and professional-grade safety and radio equipment: liferafts certified for passengers, communications matching the authorised navigation type, and sometimes a different licence for whoever skippers it. When that boat moves to list 7 for private use, all of this gets simpler and usually cheaper, both insurance and equipment upkeep. It's one of the real advantages of buying an ex-charter boat that's already past the 4-year mark: you inherit a well-equipped boat with private-owner running costs.

A worked example

Say there's a 9.5-metre boat, registered on list 6 two and a half years ago, that its owner now wants to sell to a private buyer. The tax authority values that model and year at around €95,000 on its tables. Since the 4 years since the exemption was recognised haven't passed, switching it to private use means the IEDMT has to be settled on that value: 12% of €95,000 is €11,400. If the seller asks €90,000 for the boat "free of charges" and doesn't mention this, the real buyer is paying €101,400, not €90,000. That's exactly what I check before agreeing to list an ex-charter boat.

The 4-year rule

When a boat is registered on list 6 with an IEDMT exemption, the law requires the exemption's conditions to be kept up for 4 years from the taxable event. If before that period the boat leaves charter, changes activity or is sold to a private individual for private use, the IEDMT has to be self-assessed at that point, on the boat's value at that time, not the value it had when it was registered.

Once 4 years have passed, this problem disappears: the boat can move to list 7 with nothing to settle on this account. That's why the first question I ask about any ex-charter boat is the exact date it was registered on list 6. Not the build date, not the listing date: the date the exemption was recognised.

How to switch from list 6 to list 7

The change of list is processed at the Harbour Master's Office with: an application, ID, an updated registration certificate, navigation permit and a valid certificate of seaworthiness, and deregistration of the business activity (forms 036/037). If the 4-year period has passed, there's no IEDMT to settle; if it hasn't, that has to be settled first. A simplified ITB matching the new use can be requested. The indicative processing time runs roughly 7 to 20 working days, depending on the Harbour Master's Office and its current workload; it's worth confirming with your accountant when starting the file, since it varies.

New in 2025: the temporary switch from private to commercial use

In July 2025 (Resolution of the Directorate-General of the Merchant Marine of 17/07/2025, in force since 23/07/2025), a new route opened up: a list 7 boat can switch temporarily to commercial use (charter) without permanently changing list. It requires management by a professional company, rental insurance, a special "CT" marking on the boat, and the owner can't work on board during that period.

It's a provisional regime, still awaiting development by royal decree, and the resolution doesn't spell out its tax effects precisely. I mention it because it's a legitimate selling point for a buyer who wants to offset the mooring cost by renting the boat out a few days a year without giving up private use the rest of the time. But before promising anything to a client about this option, I always check it with the accountant: it's too recent to give firm figures.

When each list makes sense, and what to check on an ex-charter boat

For a private buyer who wants to sail on weekends, list 7 is the one that matters. List 6 only makes sense if there's a genuine intention to run the boat commercially, with all the business activity, insurance and tax registration that involves. Buying a list 6 boat without that intention, just because the listed price looks good, almost always ends in the surprise of an outstanding IEDMT bill.

When I review a listing for an ex-charter boat, I always check the same things:

  1. The exact date it was registered on list 6, and whether the 4 years have already passed.
  2. Whether the listed price already discounts the outstanding IEDMT, where it applies.
  3. The state of the inspections and certificates, which tend to be stricter on list 6.
  4. Whether the seller intends to process the change of list before the sale, or whether it's left to the buyer.
