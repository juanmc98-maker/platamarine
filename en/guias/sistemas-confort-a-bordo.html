Comfort systems on board

By Juan MoranteSeptember 20268 min read

Comfort on board a boat starts with being able to sit in the shade, keep food cold and shower after a swim. Sleeping aboard for several days asks for a bit more. Before comparing extras, I'd start from actual use: what you need, what it draws, and what it costs to keep running.

Transom shower and hot water

A transom shower lets you rinse off the salt without wetting the interior. Check the tank, pump, connections and drains. Open the tap and close it: if the pump keeps running without water flowing, there may be a leak or a pressure problem.

For sleeping aboard, a 20-40 litre water heater adds real comfort. Check for corrosion, the safety valve, the heating element and its age. Some also heat via the engine's cooling circuit; not all do. Check how it's powered and how much usable water is left after a shower.

Fridge, freezer and cooker

A 12/24 V compressor fridge is usually a good priority. Ask to see it cool down, and check the seals, ventilation and corrosion. A small ice compartment doesn't necessarily equal a freezer: confirm the temperature it reaches and holds.

Consumption depends on heat, insulation and how often it's opened. As a rough calculation, a fridge averaging 25 W uses 600 Wh a day. Measuring it over a full day tells you more than listening to the compressor for a few minutes.

For simple meals, a small cooker is enough. On gas, check the installation, the age of the components, and the bottle locker with an external drain. Induction avoids gas but demands power: 1,500 W for twenty minutes is 500 Wh, before losses. Neither installation is one to improvise.

Manual or electric toilet, and black water

A marine toilet can be manual or electric. The manual kind depends less on the battery; the electric one is more convenient but adds a pump or macerator and its own consumption. Flush it several times and check for leaks, backflow, noise and smells in the pipework.

The black water tank needs proper ventilation, access and a way to empty it. Check its capacity, the level gauge, and the deck pump-out fitting. Don't confuse "has a toilet" with "has a holding tank." Ask to be shown the circuit and use the marina's pump-out facilities.

Air conditioning and heating

Air conditioning on a boat is most useful if you sleep in a hot cabin. The right choice depends on the volume, glazing and insulation, not just the length overall. Water-cooled marine systems need a sea water intake, filter, pump and condensate drain.

Test both heat and cold if it's reversible. Check cooling flow, filters, corrosion and the power draw on the unit's nameplate. Ask for maintenance invoices: the fan blowing doesn't prove the circuit is actually cooling properly.

For winter, a diesel air heater can suit better than relying on 230 V shore power. It requires a marine-rated installation, a sealed exhaust, and a fuel system check. Avoid unvented combustion heaters indoors.

Generator, lithium, inverter and charging

Generator versus batteries

A marine generator can sustain high loads while it's running, at the cost of fuel, noise and maintenance. Check hours and age, cold start, exhaust, cooling, and performance under load. Don't judge it just because it starts. A portable petrol generator is no substitute for a proper marine installation.

Lithium stores energy; the inverter converts it to 230 V. Installing it means checking the BMS, fuses, cable sizing, chargers and alternator compatibility. It's not a simple battery swap. Ask for the wiring diagram and invoices for the installation.

A calculation before buying

A 12.8 V, 200 Ah battery stores 2.56 kWh nominal. With 80% usable and a 90%-efficient inverter, it would deliver roughly 1.84 kWh. A constant 1,000 W load would last about 1.8 hours, before counting anything else. This is an example, not a guaranteed runtime.

Shore power charger and solar panels

Check the charger has the right battery profile set, and that the shore power connection, residual current device and protections all work. Solar panels help with the fridge and lights; the bimini's shade and the available surface area limit how much they produce. Check the mounts, connectors and regulator. Don't count on the rated output all day long.

The Victron lithium installation documentation covers integration with the BMS and charging systems in detail; the whole system should be sized by a professional.

Watermaker: range with maintenance

A boat watermaker desalinates sea water by reverse osmosis. It pays off when you spend days away from a tap. If you're back in port every evening, it usually adds more cost and maintenance than it's worth.

Check litres per hour and power draw for the specific model. Ask for a demonstration of output and salinity, filter history and how the membranes are preserved during layups. Clear water doesn't mean drinkable water: the cleanliness of the tank and circuit matters too. Alamar publishes units of different flow rates; the unit's price doesn't necessarily include installation or VAT.

Bimini, enclosures, platform and ladder

A bimini that actually covers where you sit tends to get more use than many pricier extras. Check the fabric, stitching, zips and fittings. Enclosures extend the season, but they need to allow ventilation without trapping condensation.

Try the ladder from the water: depth, grip and rung spacing all matter. On the platform, check for play, cracks and repairs at the mounting points. An extension needs the structure, weight and any paperwork implications checked before you order it.

What's worth it, by use

  • 6-8 m, day trips: shade, ladder, shower and cold storage. A generator and watermaker are rarely a priority.
  • 8-11 m, weekends: toilet with a holding tank, fridge, hot water and a well-sized house battery.
  • 11-15 m, holidays: weigh up climate control and range based on nights at anchor, number aboard and access to port.

On a used boat, ask for the age, invoices and a test of every piece of equipment. Round it out with what to check when buying. If you're unsure about length or layout, start with the What Boat Do I Need quiz and message me on WhatsApp from the contact link on this page.

Frequently asked questions

Can you have air conditioning on a boat without a generator?

Yes, connected to shore power or with batteries and an inverter sized for it. DC-powered units also exist. Runtime depends on real consumption, usable battery capacity and other loads.

Is a watermaker worth it on an 8-metre boat?

Usually not for day trips or nights with a frequent return to port. It can make sense for long stays at anchor, if there's space, power and water consumption that justifies it.

Is a manual or electric marine toilet better?

Both are marine-grade. Manual simplifies installation and works without electricity; electric is more comfortable but adds consumption and components. The condition of the pipework and tank matters as much as the toilet itself.

Do solar panels replace the boat's generator?

They can cover part of the load from the fridge, lights and small equipment. For air conditioning or an electric hob you need to calculate output, storage and consumption: available surface area is usually the main limit.

Do extras pay for themselves when you sell the boat?

Not necessarily. What matters is that they fit the boat's use and work well. An old, unmaintained piece of equipment can become a cost for the buyer instead of adding value.
