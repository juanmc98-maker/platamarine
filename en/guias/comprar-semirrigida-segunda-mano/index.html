Buying a used RIB: what to check before you pay

By Juan MoranteUpdated October 20268 min read

On a used RIB, the year of the hull tells you little. What really sets the price, and causes the headaches, is the tubes, the engine and the paperwork. This is what I check before recommending one, with the details of the RIBs I have for sale right now.

What drives the price of a used RIB

More than the hull year, what counts is the engine (its year, its hours and how it's been serviced), the condition of the tubes, the equipment and whether it comes with a trailer. An example from my listings: the Sacs 535 is from 2002, but the owner has fitted new tubes and upholstery, painted the hull and replaced the wiring and batteries. That's why its price looks nothing like that of another RIB from the same year with everything original.

The tubes: the first thing I look at

They take the most punishment (sun, rubbing against the pontoon, the trailer) and, after the engine, they're the most expensive part to repair or replace.

  • Material. They're usually PVC-coated or CSM-coated fabric (what everyone calls Hypalon). Hypalon usually stands up to the sun better, but the quality of the fabric, how the tubes were made and how they've been looked after matter too. Ask which one it has and how old the tubes are: the ones on the Sacs, for example, are new PVC.
  • Pressure. Ask for them to be inflated the day before and check them, ideally with a gauge, compartment by compartment. Bear in mind that pressure drops by itself when it's cold, so a slightly softer tube doesn't on its own mean it's leaking. If it really is losing air, the leak needs finding: valves, seams or fabric.
  • Seams and bonding. Check where the tube joins the hull, the stern cones and the rub areas. Plenty of RIBs have patches and that isn't always a problem; bonding that's coming apart is.
  • Appearance. Faded, sticky to the touch or with fine cracks: signs of ageing that call for a closer look.
  • Valves. All present and sealing properly; each compartment has its own.

If the tubes are original and old, look especially carefully at the seams, bonding and fabric. If they're in poor shape, get a workshop quote to repair or replace them before you make an offer: it's a significant cost.

The engine and the hours

Almost all used RIBs have outboards. What I check:

  • The engine year isn't the hull year. On the Sacs, 2002 hull and 2007 engine; on the Tiger Marine 650, 2015 hull and 2012 engine. Read the hours against the engine's year.
  • Hours that match the boat's story. The Tiger Marine's owner reports around 110 hours and says it's only in the water in August, spending the rest of the year stored indoors. That kind of use fits low hours, but it's still worth checking them, where possible, with the engine diagnostics and the invoices. Low hours are good if the engine has been looked after; an engine that has sat for years without servicing has its own problems.
  • Invoices. Oil, filters, water pump impeller, anodes and gearcase oil. If there's no paperwork, ask who did the servicing.
  • Cold start. Ask to see it start without having been run earlier that day. Check water comes out of the tell-tale, no alarm goes off and the temperature stays normal: the tell-tale stream alone doesn't prove the whole cooling circuit is working.
  • Gearcase, propeller and controls. No water in the gearcase oil, no damage to the propeller, and smooth trim and steering.
  • A mechanic, if in doubt. Have one you trust check it before you pay. A compression test helps spot internal problems (especially if one cylinder reads very differently from the rest), but it doesn't replace a full inspection.

Hull, console and transom

  • Keel and bottom: knocks from beaching or from the trailer.
  • Transom: solid around the engine bolts, with no cracks, damp or signs of movement. A transom can be damaged inside without visibly flexing, and repairing it is serious work.
  • Repairs: if the hull has been painted or repaired, ask what was done and who did it.
  • Console and seats: firmly fixed, no cracks in the fibreglass.
  • Electrics and equipment: navigation lights, bilge pump, VHF and anything else fitted. Everything should work with the engine running.

Paperwork, licences and taxes

  • Owner and charges. If the boat is Spanish-flagged, ask for an up-to-date registry extract (hoja de asiento): it shows the registry details and any entries against the boat. If it's foreign-flagged, like the Tiger Marine (Polish register), you need to check the equivalent documents from that country and its ownership history. What a Polish flag means is explained in this guide.
  • Licence. What counts is the hull length in the documents, not the model name. In Spain, the Licencia de Navegación lets you skipper boats up to 6 metres with the engine power the manufacturer specifies as suitable, in daylight only and no more than 2 miles from a port, marina or shelter. The PNB covers motorboats up to 8 metres, no more than 5 miles out. Above that, a higher licence. That's why the Sacs (5.35 m) needs the Licencia de Navegación and the Tiger Marine (about 6.50 m) the PNB. See licences.
  • ITB (inspection). For a Spanish boat on List 7 (private use), there are no periodic inspections under 6 metres; from 6 to 24 metres, at most every 5 years. On List 6 (charter), always. Ask for the certificate of seaworthiness and any inspections due; for a foreign-flagged boat, whatever its country requires. It's explained in the ITB guide.
  • Taxes. If you buy from a private seller, you normally pay transfer tax (ITP), and the rate depends on the region. If a company or professional sells as part of their business, the sale normally carries VAT. Buying through a broker doesn't turn a private sale into a business sale. Each case is confirmed with the paperwork before you reserve; the rates are in the guide to ITP on a used boat.
  • Insurance. Third-party liability insurance is compulsory. Check the policy is in force and covers the boat and the use you'll give it.

If it comes with a trailer

Many RIBs are sold with a trailer (both of mine, for example). What to check:

  • Papers. Above 750 kg gross weight, the trailer has its own registration, ITV and insurance, and ownership has to be transferred too.
  • Your licence. In Spain, a B licence lets you tow a trailer up to 750 kg gross weight. Above 750 kg, with a B licence the gross weights of car and trailer together can't exceed 3,500 kg; up to 4,250 kg you need B96, and above that, BE.
  • Your car. Check its registration documents for how much it can tow.
  • Condition. Lights, rollers, tyres, winch and a chassis without serious rust.

If you need a new one, there are the trailers I order from the factory.

The sea trial

Whenever possible, take it out with a normal load: how it gets onto the plane, whether the engine reaches the manufacturer's full-throttle rev range, any vibration or odd noises, any alarm or rising temperature, and how it turns. Back in port, check the tubes again and whether any water has come in.

What if you'd rather have a new one?

This guide is for guidance only and is based on what I check when I look at a RIB; it doesn't replace an inspection by a mechanic or a surveyor. The prices are the asking prices of the boats I have for sale, not a market table, and hours and equipment are reported by the owner and checked against the paperwork before you reserve.
