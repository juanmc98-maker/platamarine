What a yacht broker does and what they charge

By Juan MoranteUpdated September 20268 min read

"Yacht broker" is a term used for quite different things. Here I explain what I do in a sale, what I do when I'm searching for a boat for a buyer, what's typically charged in the industry, and how my model works at Plata Marine.

What a broker does in a sale

The work starts before the listing goes live and doesn't end until the money and the boat have both changed hands:

  • Valuation, comparing against real closing prices, not just listing prices, which are almost always above what actually ends up being paid.
  • Listing sheet and photos: a full write-up of the boat, technical details, engine hours, equipment, real condition, including what needs improving (it builds more trust than hiding it).
  • Publishing on specialist portals and reaching out to the registered buyer network.
  • Filtering buyers: separating the curious from real buyers before setting up a viewing.
  • Viewings and sea trials, coordinated with the owner.
  • Negotiation between the parties, backed by data, not just gut feel.
  • Paperwork: I coordinate with the admin agent on the registry sheet, the ITP, the change of ownership and, where it applies, the IEDMT.
  • Closing: purchase contract, payment and handover.

What a broker does on a search for a buyer

It's the same work, from the other side: I work with you to narrow down the type of boat, length, budget and what you'll actually use it for (I cover this in the guide to types of motor boat), I scan the market, including private listings that aren't always on portals, filter out what doesn't fit, check paperwork and condition before you waste time on a viewing (using the checklist in what to check when buying a used boat), and I negotiate on your behalf.

What's usually charged

In Europe the norm is a commission of between 8 and 10% plus VAT on the sale price, with a minimum so small deals don't run at a loss. In the United States the reference is 10%, almost always with several months of exclusivity. When two brokers are involved in a deal, the commission is split between them. Those are the industry figures; mine are lower, and I give them to you in writing before we start.

Exclusive or not

An exclusive listing means only that broker can sell the boat during a set period, even if the owner finds a buyer on their own. In return, the broker invests more in photos, video and advertising. It's the standard model in the US and with several of the big European brokers, and it has a logic I agree with: a boat listed at the same time by three brokers, with three prices and three different write-ups, burns out. Buyers see it repeated, suspect something's off, and haggle harder.

I don't ask for exclusivity, but I do ask for what prevents that problem: one price, one listing sheet. If the owner also advertises it themselves or with someone else, it's at the same price with the same information. And I work the sale in stages: price and paperwork first, then photos and the listing, then filtering buyers and viewings. That order is what makes a boat sell well, exclusive or not.

The Plata Marine model

I work on success, with no exclusivity and no upfront fees:

  • A percentage of the sale price, below the European average, with a minimum for small deals. I give it to you in writing before we start; no small print.
  • No exclusivity: you can sell on your own or with someone else, and if you sell it yourself directly, you pay me nothing.
  • No upfront fees or monthly charges: I only get paid if there's a sale.
  • For buyers, a search works the same way, on success. And if the boat you choose is from my own portfolio for sale, you don't pay a search fee: I'm already being paid on the seller's side.

I can keep the percentage below the European average because I work without a dockside office and without the structure of a big brokerage: what there is, is time dedicated to each boat and each buyer, not fixed costs to pass on.

The exact figures for my fees I give over WhatsApp or in person, depending on the boat, before starting work and in writing. What never changes is that the ITP, the admin agent and the fees are paid by the buyer and aren't part of my commission.

What a broker does NOT do

  • Not a surveyor: I can recommend an independent survey, but I don't replace a professional's technical report.
  • Not an admin agent: I coordinate paperwork with the boating admin agent, but registration, ITP and IEDMT procedures are handled by a licensed agent.
  • Doesn't guarantee the boat: responsibility for the boat's condition lies with the seller, not the broker. That's why the paperwork, inventory and declared condition are put in writing in the contract.

How to choose a broker

Ask clearly: what commission do they charge and on what basis, is there a minimum, do they ask for exclusivity and what does it actually involve, do they charge anything upfront (if they do, be wary: the industry norm is to charge only on a sale), what's included in the service (photos, portals, paperwork handling), and which admin agent they work with for the procedures.

The commissions and figures in this guide are the industry norm based on public information and reference estimates; many brokers don't publish their exact percentage and negotiate it case by case. The Plata Marine model described is the one I apply; any particular terms are put in writing before starting work.
