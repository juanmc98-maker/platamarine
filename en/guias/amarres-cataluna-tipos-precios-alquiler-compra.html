Moorings in Catalonia: types, how they work and what they really cost

By Juan MoranteSeptember 2026Rates checked in September 202614 min read

When someone asks me how much it costs to own a boat, the mooring is usually the expense that catches them out most. It depends on the harbour, on length times beam, on whether you rent or buy a right of use, and on a handful of extras that don't show up in the headline rate. Here I explain it the way I wish someone had explained it to me, with the official rates for the Catalan coast in hand.

What a mooring is and what it includes

A mooring is a spot in the water, next to a pontoon or a quay, with the right to leave the boat there. It's measured in metres of length by metres of beam, which is why two 8-metre boats can pay different rates if one is wider. Normally the price covers the spot, access to the pontoon, security and the harbour's basic services (toilets, showers, parking in some cases). Water and electricity are included in some harbours and billed by consumption in others, and that changes the final bill quite a bit.

There are two types of stay. A home-based boat is one that has the harbour as its base, with an authorised stay of six months or more; Catalan law defines it that way. A transient boat is one stopping for a few days, and pays a much higher daily rate, especially in July and August.

The four types of harbour you'll come across

In Catalonia almost all marinas fall under the Generalitat via Ports de la Generalitat, except Barcelona and Tarragona, which are state harbours. But what matters to you as a user isn't who owns the harbour, it's who manages it and how they charge you.

Directly managed harbours

Run by the Generalitat itself: Palamós, Blanes, L'Estartit, Sant Feliu de Guíxols, the public basin at Roses, Llançà or L'Escala, among others. There's no renting or buying here: you pay a public fee, the TA5, calculated by square metres occupied per day. These are the cheapest moorings on the coast, which is exactly why you have to apply and wait for a spot. There's no public waiting list you can check; you apply with the Ports form and they contact you as spots come up.

Marinas under concession

These are harbours built and run by a private company for the length of a concession: Port Ginesta, Marina Empuriabrava, Port de Sitges-Aiguadolç, Port Masnou, Marina Palamós, Port d'Aro or Port de Roses, for example. The company sets its own rental rates and can also transfer moorings until the concession ends, which is what people call "buying". They tend to have more services (dockhands, boatyard, restaurants) and higher prices than the public ones.

Nautical clubs

Many Catalan harbours are run by a non-profit nautical club holding the concession: Arenys, El Balís, Vilanova, Cambrils, Salou, L'Escala, L'Estartit, Sant Feliu or La Ràpita. They work like a marina, but with one difference: to hold a mooring on member terms you normally have to join, with an entry fee and a monthly fee. In exchange, the mooring price is usually lower than for non-members, and you get a pool, restaurant, sailing school and club atmosphere. Arenys, for example, publishes a 3,000-euro entry fee and 95 euros a month in membership, on top of the mooring.

State harbours and special cases

Marina Port Vell and Marina Vela sit inside the Port of Barcelona, and Port Tarraco inside the Port of Tarragona; these are aimed at large yachts and don't publish rates for boats our size. Port Olímpic is a case of its own: it belongs to the Generalitat, but since 2020 it has been run by Barcelona City Council through B:SM, with use transferred for a monthly fee. And Port de Mataró is run by a public consortium that tenders mooring concessions for 10, 15 or 30 years.

Renting or buying: what each one actually means

This is the part that causes the most confusion, and it's worth having it clear before signing anything.

Renting is what it sounds like: a contract, usually annual with a monthly or seasonal option, and a rate based on length and beam. It's the most flexible option and the one I recommend to anyone who has just bought their first boat, or isn't sure they'll want a different length in two years.

Buying doesn't really exist as such. Harbours are public domain, and Catalan harbour law states explicitly that this domain is inalienable: it cannot be sold. What you actually buy is a transfer of the preferential right of use of a mooring until the harbour's concession ends. That right can be resold, inherited or rented to a third party, with two conditions that repeat across almost every set of harbour rules: you must notify the concession holder, who usually has a right of first refusal, and you must be up to date with payments. When the concession ends, the harbour reverts to the administration and the right of use is extinguished along with it; the law doesn't guarantee the new concession holder will honour anything. That's why the most important question when buying a mooring isn't how much it costs, but how many years of concession are left.

Always ask for the date

Some examples that are actually published: the Marina Vela (Barcelona) concession expires on 9 May 2044; Port del Garraf's, as published when awarded, runs from 2020 to 2045; Club Nàutic Cambrils was selling its last moorings in 2021 with a concession running to 2043; Port Masnou's concession had expired and been extended to 2027, pending a new award. The year 2036 comes up a lot in Port Ginesta listings, but I haven't found it in an official source, so ask the harbour in writing before trusting it. Catalan law also sets a maximum of 25 years for new concessions (50 only for works of exceptional importance), which gives an idea of where terms are heading.

On top of the transfer price, the holder keeps paying a yearly maintenance or community fee to the harbour, plus utilities and charges. At Port Olímpic that's 2.50 euros per square metre a month; at Arenys the holder of an 8-metre mooring pays 140 euros a month in maintenance; at Empuriabrava a fee proportional to the water surface. A bought mooring isn't a free mooring.

What determines the price

Four things, in this order: surface area (length times beam; some harbours charge by square metre, others by length bracket), the harbour and its area (Barcelona and the Maresme are pricier than the Ebro; the Costa Brava sits in the middle, with Empuriabrava and Palamós at the top), the regime (member or non-member, home-based or transient) and the season, which for transient boats can triple the price between March and August. In home-based renting the season barely matters; for transient stays it's everything.

Three notes on the table. Port Masnou and Club Nàutic Cambrils publish their rate on their website without stating the year, so I take whichever is current when the guide is reviewed. Port de Mataró works differently: the Consorci tenders mooring concessions and the holder pays the Generalitat's TA5 fee plus a monthly maintenance charge approved in the DOGC; the figure I give is the sum of the two. And Sant Carles Marina only publishes boatyard rates, with moorings quoted individually. If you're at a harbour that isn't listed and you have the rate to hand, I'd be grateful if you passed it on.

Two quick takeaways. First: for an 8-metre boat the range runs from around 1,800 euros a year at a public harbour to over 8,000 in an all-inclusive Costa Brava package, and most marinas and clubs in the Maresme and Garraf sit between 2,700 and 3,700 plus VAT and utilities. Second: at 12 metres the jump is big, because the surface area nearly doubles; budget 5,000 to 8,000 euros a year in most places.

What a right of use costs

Transfer prices usually aren't published, so you have to go harbour by harbour. The ones that are public give a reference: Club Nàutic Vilanova sells at 550 euros per square metre plus VAT with membership entry included, which for an 8 × 3.10 works out to around 16,500 euros and for a 12 × 4.20 around 33,500; Club Nàutic la Ràpita publishes 20,000 euros for 8 × 3 and 30,000 for 12 × 4, VAT included; Club Nàutic Cambrils was offering its last moorings in 2021 at 1,200 euros per square metre with a concession to 2043; and Port de Mataró tendered in 2014 between 441 and 550 euros per square metre plus VAT depending on whether the concession was for 10, 15 or 30 years. On the second-hand market, listings in the Barcelona area run from 20,000 to 30,000 euros for 10 to 12 metres, and in Empuriabrava they range from around 20,000 on the inner canals to well over 100,000 for moorings with direct access to the sea, which there are sold together with the plot.

Two warnings. The price always has to be read alongside how many years of concession are left: 25,000 euros for 10 years works out to 2,500 a year plus the maintenance fee, and with that you can already compare it to renting. And the tax side: if a company or the club transfers it to you, the deal carries 21% VAT; if a private individual transfers it, transfer tax applies, and the tax authorities have been treating these transfers as a transfer of real estate, which in Catalonia can mean the general property rate. Before signing, have your accountant confirm which rate applies in your case.

The costs almost no one counts

  • Water and electricity. When billed by consumption, published prices range from 3.30 to 6 euros per cubic metre of water and 0.18 to 0.51 euros per kilowatt hour. On an 8-metre boat with normal use it isn't much; with air conditioning and a fridge running all summer, it is.
  • Membership fee. At clubs, an entry fee and a monthly fee on top of the mooring. Ask whether the entry fee is refunded if you leave (usually not).
  • T0 navigation aids fee. For motorboats of 9 metres or more (12 for sailboats), 9.12 euros per square metre a year, and almost every harbour bills it separately.
  • Third-party liability insurance. Compulsory for any motorboat and for sailboats over 6 metres, with minimum cover of 120,202 euros per victim, 240,404 per incident and 96,162 for material damage. Harbours require it as a condition of mooring and will ask for proof every year.
  • Annual haul-out. Lifting the boat out, cleaning and painting the hull below the waterline and putting it back in: budget for the crane (Port Olímpic publishes €9.18 per metre of length per lift) plus the time on the hard and the work itself. I cover it in the end-of-season guide.
  • Maintenance on a bought mooring. The annual community or maintenance fee mentioned above.
  • Dockhands and services. Help berthing, changing fenders, checking lines before a storm: included at some harbours, charged by the hour at others (Roses publishes 63 to 100 euros an hour).

Alternatives to a classic mooring

Dry storage. The boat lives on land, on a rack or a yard, and is launched when you call ahead. For boats up to 8 or 9 metres with an outboard it's a very reasonable option: Marina Seca de Horta, in Barcelona, publishes 1,284 euros a year for 7.5 to 8 metres; at Generalitat harbours the dry-storage fee is roughly half the afloat rate. The boat is kept in much better condition out of the water, without antifouling or osmosis, in exchange for having to call ahead before heading out.

Buoys and anchorages. Some harbours offer seasonal or nightly buoys: Roses publishes 18.50 euros a night for 6 to 10 metres outside July and August, and 39 in high summer. Useful for a few days here and there, not as a base.

Trailer. Up to 7 metres, many owners keep the boat at home or in a car park and use the club ramp (free for members at Arenys, 45 euros per use for non-members). It's the cheapest option and the most work.

How to choose a harbour

My advice is to start with how you'll actually use the boat, not the price. If you go out every weekend, a harbour more than an hour from home will kill your motivation; if you only use it on holiday, you can afford to go further and save money. After that, ask for the full rate in writing with what it includes, ask about the waiting list and the terms for leaving, and if you're buying a right of use, ask for the concession's end date and the maintenance fee before you even talk price. And visit the harbour on a Saturday in July: you see things the rate sheet doesn't show.

If you're boat shopping and aren't sure what length suits you, this tool helps you narrow it down in two minutes, and the type of mooring you need pretty much follows from that.
