Mandatory safety equipment on board:
what to carry by navigation zone (Spain 2026)

By Juan Morante · October 2026 · 8 min read

Buying a second-hand boat only to discover it is missing flares, that the fire extinguisher has expired or that the life jackets are not the right type is more common than you might think. Knowing in advance what the law requires lets you negotiate the price, ask the seller to complete the equipment before signing, or calculate the real cost of the boat from day one. This guide covers what current Spanish regulations require — Royal Decree 339/2021, of 18 May — in a single table you can consult before buying or before each outing.

The 7 navigation zones in Spain

Spanish regulations classify waters into seven zones according to distance from the coast or a refuge port. The higher the zone (lower number), the more equipment the law requires. Zone 7 covers sheltered inland waters and harbours; Zone 1 is unlimited oceanic navigation.

Zone 6Up to 2 nm from coast
Zone 5Up to 5 nm
Zone 4Up to 12 nm
Zone 3Up to 25 nm
Zone 2Up to 60 nm
Zone 1Unlimited distance

The vast majority of motor recreational boats between 6 and 12 metres navigating along the Catalan coast operate in zones 4 to 6 (between 2 and 12 nautical miles from the coast). The boat's navigation dispatch document — processed by the Maritime Authority or, for national outings, kept in order by the skipper — specifies the zone and maximum number of persons for which the vessel is authorised.

Life jackets: buoyancy, marking and inspection

The minimum buoyancy (in Newtons) required by law depends on the zone for which the vessel is dispatched:

  • 100 N — zones 5, 6 and 7 (navigation up to 5 nm). Keeps a conscious person face-up, but does not turn an unconscious person over.
  • 150 N — zones 3 and 4 (up to 25 nm). Turns an unconscious person face-up and keeps the head above water.
  • 275 N — zones 1 and 2 (oceanic). Greater buoyancy and survival time in rough water or integrated survival suits.

All life jackets must carry CE marking with the corresponding standard. Jackets for zones 3 and beyond must incorporate a self-activating flash light that activates on immersion. Those for zones 1 and 2 must also carry a whistle and reflector.

Inflatable life jackets — the most commonly used today for their comfort — require annual inspection of the trigger mechanism and CO₂ cylinder. When buying a second-hand boat, check that each jacket has a valid inspection label and that the cylinder has not been punctured.

Flares and pyrotechnic signals: expiry and disposal

Marine flares expire three years from the date of manufacture (or on the date indicated on the packaging). After that date they are invalid during an inspection, even if they may technically still function.

For zone 4 (the most common along the Catalan coast, up to 12 nm) the minimum requirement is:

  • 3 hand flares (red)
  • 3 parachute rocket flares (red)

For zones 1, 2 and 3 the equipment rises to 6 of each type, with floating orange smoke signals added from zone 3 onwards.

Expired flares must not be thrown in the rubbish. Some marinas have collection points; otherwise contact the Maritime Authority or your municipality's hazardous waste service. Always keep expired flares separate from new ones to avoid mixing them up.

Fire extinguisher on board: how many and what type

A fire extinguisher is mandatory on every powered vessel, regardless of navigation zone. The minimum quantity depends on the vessel's length, not the zone:

  • Under 15 m: 1 portable 34 B extinguisher (dry powder ABC or marine CO₂)
  • 15 to 20 m: 2 × 34 B extinguishers
  • 20 to 24 m: 3 × 34 B extinguishers

Outboards up to 7.5 kW on vessels under 6 m may be exempt in certain circumstances; if in doubt, consult your Maritime Authority. Vessels with inboard or integrated engines must also have the extinguisher accessible from the cockpit, not locked below decks out of reach.

Extinguishers require periodic inspection (generally annually for dry powder types). Check the inspection label and the condition of the seal when buying a used boat: an out-of-date extinguisher may cause problems during the ITB technical inspection or a Maritime Civil Guard check.

First aid kit

Order FOM/1254/2002 regulates the medical kit for recreational vessels with paid crew. For private vessels without professional crew on board, regulations do not specify the minimum contents, but the vessel must carry adequate first aid material.

For typical coastal navigation in zones 4 to 6, a reasonable first aid kit includes:

  • Analgesics (ibuprofen or paracetamol)
  • Antihistamine for insect bites or allergic reactions
  • Dressings, gauze and bandages in various sizes
  • Saline solution for wound cleaning
  • Antiseptic (chlorhexidine or povidone-iodine)
  • Blunt-tipped scissors and tweezers
  • Latex or vinyl gloves
  • Emergency thermal blanket

When buying a second-hand boat, check the expiry dates on the medicines in the first aid kit: it is very common to find them all past their use-by date. A basic restock costs no more than €30–50.

VHF and EPIRB: not mandatory, but potentially life-saving

Royal Decree 339/2021 does not require a VHF radio or EPIRB on private recreational vessels. However, their absence can be decisive in an emergency.

A marine VHF lets you communicate on channel 16 (the distress and permanent watch channel of Salvamento Marítimo) and coordinate with other vessels. A certified portable marine unit costs between €80 and €250. From zone 4 onwards it is highly recommended; in zone 3 or further, practically essential. If you navigate at night or in reduced visibility, consider carrying one even close to shore.

An EPIRB radiobeacon transmits a distress signal at 406 MHz that the COSPAS-SARSAT system relays to rescue centres. To be of any use it must be registered with SASEMAR (the Spanish Maritime Rescue and Safety Society) with the vessel's details; without registration, it may generate a false alarm without leading anyone to a rescue. Registration is free on their website. Note that the EPIRB inspection interval is two years.

A PLB (Personal Locator Beacon) works like an EPIRB but is worn on the person; useful on sailing boats or vessels where falling overboard is a real possibility.

What to ask the seller before signing the purchase agreement

When I evaluate a second-hand boat for a client, the first thing we look at is the documentation and safety equipment. Specific points to check:

  • The navigation dispatch document: confirms what zone the boat is authorised for and the maximum number of persons. If the current equipment does not cover that zone, it must be updated before leaving port.
  • The life jackets: sufficient number for the maximum crew on the dispatch, correct type for the zone, valid CE marking and — if inflatable — annual service up to date.
  • The flares: check the expiry date on each package. Having the right number means nothing if they have expired.
  • The fire extinguisher: valid inspection label and intact seal.
  • The most recent ITB (Technical Boat Inspection): this checks that the vessel met its mandatory equipment requirements. If deficiencies are recorded, ask for them to be remedied before the sale.

Incomplete or expired equipment is not necessarily a deal-breaker; it is a negotiating argument on the price. Replacing a complete kit for zone 4 on a vessel of 8 to 10 metres (150 N life jackets, flares, extinguisher and lifebuoy) costs approximately €400–800 depending on brands and how much the boat already has in good condition.
