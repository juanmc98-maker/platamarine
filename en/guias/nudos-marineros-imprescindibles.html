8 sailing knots you'll actually use, explained step by step

By Juan MoranteSeptember 202610 min read

You don't need to know fifty knots to handle a boat properly. With these eight, done right, you can cover mooring, anchoring, joining two lines of different thickness, and getting out of trouble without the knot jamming or coming loose on its own. Explained the way I'd teach someone who just bought their first boat: no Latin names, no extra steps.

Before getting into it, an idea I repeat a lot: a good sailing knot does three things. It holds the load you're using it for, it doesn't tighten so much that you can't undo it later with cold hands, and it's easy to check at a glance. The eight that follow do all three. Practise them first at home with a metre of line; on the dock, in a hurry with wind blowing, is not the best time to learn.

1

Bowline

Photo: US Coast Guard / Wikimedia Commons

The most useful knot on board. It forms a fixed loop that won't tighten or slip no matter how hard you pull on it, yet it still comes undone with one hand the moment you take the load off. I use it to moor to a bollard when I need a big loop, to hoist something by hooking it through the loop, or whenever I want an eye that won't pinch the line.

  1. With the tail (the free end) make a small loop over the standing part (the part that stays fixed), as if drawing a sideways "letter P".
  2. Pass the tail upward through that loop, "coming out of the hole".
  3. Take the tail around behind the standing part and feed it back down through the same hole it came out of.
  4. Pull the standing part and the big loop at the same time so the knot closes and sits snug.

The trick I always teach: "the rabbit comes out of the hole, goes round the tree, and back down the hole."

2

Clove hitch

Photo: US Coast Guard / Wikimedia Commons

Two crossed turns around a post, a bollard or a rail. It takes three seconds to tie, holds well while the tension stays constant, and that's why you'll see it all day on the docks for fenders, awnings or temporary moorings. Don't use it as a main mooring line for hard pulls or when the boat is left alone: if the line goes slack for a moment, it can slip.

  1. Pass the line in front of the post and cross the tail over the standing part, forming an X.
  2. Take a second turn around the post, parallel to the first.
  3. Before tensioning, tuck the tail under that second turn, between the line and the post.
  4. Pull the standing part: the two turns bind against each other and the knot stays put.
3

Square knot (reef knot)

Photo: US Coast Guard / Wikimedia Commons

Joins the two ends of a single line, or two lines of equal thickness, in a flat, symmetrical knot. It's the one you tie around a parcel or to hold a tarp down, not for anything that's going to hang under real force: under load, or with two lines of different thickness, it can slip and let go suddenly, so for real work I'd rather use the sheet bend below.

  1. Cross the left tail over the right one and pass it underneath, like the first step of tying your shoelaces.
  2. Now cross the tail that ended up on the right over the one on the left, and pass it underneath too.
  3. Pull both standing parts at once and check you're left with two symmetrical loops, one on each side: if one tail ends up loose and parallel to the standing part (instead of crossed), you've tied a "granny knot" and it will come undone on its own.
4

Sheet bend

Photo: US Coast Guard / Wikimedia Commons

The one I actually use to join two lines of different thickness, or a halyard to a cringle: much more secure than a square knot and almost as quick. It also works to tie a line to a ring or to an existing eye.

  1. With the thicker line (or the cringle), form a U (a bight), holding the crossing point with your fingers.
  2. Pass the tail of the thinner line up through that U from below.
  3. Wrap it around both sides of the U from behind.
  4. Tuck the tail under itself, where it first went in, and pull both standing parts to close it.

If both lines are much thinner or will be in the water, double the wrap (pass the tail through twice) for extra security: that's called a double sheet bend.

5

Round turn and two half hitches

Photo: US Coast Guard / Wikimedia Commons

A round turn around the post or eye followed by two half hitches. This is the classic quick mooring for anchoring, tying off a fender or securing a line to a ring; it copes fine with tension coming and going and checks at a glance, but it's worth finishing it off with a bowline or an extra hitch if the line will be under strain for a long time.

  1. Take a full turn around the post or pass the line through the ring.
  2. Cross the tail over the standing part and pass it behind and through the loop that forms: that's the first half hitch.
  3. Repeat the crossing a bit further down (or up) for the second half hitch, in the same direction as the first.
  4. Slide both half hitches together toward the post and pull the standing part.
6

Rolling hitch

Photo: US Coast Guard / Wikimedia Commons

Tied around another line or an anchor line that's already under tension, and it holds without slipping in the direction you pull, but slides by hand if you pull the other way. I use it to rig a snubber on the anchor, to hang a line off a lifeline, or to tension a halyard that's come up short.

  1. Take a turn around the base line and cross the tail over the standing part.
  2. Take a second turn, this time letting it sit on the inside, snug against the first, in the direction you'll be pulling.
  3. Finish with a normal half hitch, crossing on the outside, to lock the previous two.
  4. Tension in the direction of the snug turns: that's where it holds without slipping.
7

Bowline on a bight

Photo: US Coast Guard / Wikimedia Commons

The version of the bowline tied with a bight, meaning the line doubled over without using either end. It forms two fixed loops instead of one. This is the rescue knot: you pass it under a person's arms in the water, one loop for each leg, or use it to hoist something with two attachment points without it tipping off balance.

  1. Fold the line in half so you have a long bight to work with as if it were a single thick line.
  2. Make the small loop just as in the regular bowline, with the bight acting as the "tail".
  3. Pass the folded end of the bight up through the hole, open it out and take it around the front and back of the whole knot, like a loop that encloses the lot.
  4. Pull both standing parts to close it: you're left with two fixed loops, one on each side of the central knot.
8

Figure-eight knot

Photo: Just plain Bill / Wikimedia Commons

The simplest of the eight, and the one that needs the least explaining twice. It's a stopper knot: a lump at the end of a line so it can't slip out of a block, a ring or your hand at a winch, and to mark the end of a sheet. It doesn't hold load in line, it just bulks up, but it's the first one I teach anyone who's never tied a line before.

  1. Make a simple loop with the tail over the standing part.
  2. Pass the tail behind the standing part.
  3. Bring it forward through the first loop.
  4. Pull both ends to close the "eight".

A well-kept line makes better knots

No knot makes up for a line that's dried out, stiff or with a broken core inside: it slips, doesn't grip the same, and can fool the eye. If yours has spent more than two or three seasons out in the weather, check it before trusting it on a serious mooring, especially the one you use for the anchor or the tow.

These eight knots cover the vast majority of what you need on board a leisure boat. Practise them on dry land until they come without thinking: on the water, with wind or in a hurry, your hands have to know them on their own.
