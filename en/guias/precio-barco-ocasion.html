How to price a used boat (and why the listing price isn't the sale price)

By Juan MoranteUpdated September 20267 min read

When someone asks me how much their boat is worth, they almost always have in mind the price of a similar listing they've seen on a portal. The problem is that figure is what the seller is asking, not what anyone has actually paid. This guide explains the difference, how to properly value a used boat, and what really pushes the price up or down.

Listing price and closing price aren't the same thing

The listing price is an expectation. The closing price is what two people have agreed to sign. There's usually a gap between the two, and in the used market asking prices sit systematically above the final price: that's true across Europe, not just here. A listing that's sat at the same price for months isn't a market data point, it's the seller's wishful thinking.

That's why, when I value a boat, I don't just look at active listings. I look at what's actually sold, at what price and in how long. That's the difference between a valuation and an opinion.

Real comparables, not stray listings

A good comparable is the same model or a very close one, a similar year, similar engine, and above all, a closed sale price, not a listing price. To build them I check several sources:

  • Spanish portals: Cosas de Barcos, along with its associated network, is one of the most used in the domestic market; also Wallapop for the more private-seller segment.
  • European portals: YachtWorld (Boats Group) to compare against France, the UK or northern Europe; Band of Boats, from the Beneteau group, is especially useful if the boat is a Beneteau or a Jeanneau, since it cross-references thousands of valuations.
  • My own record of closed deals: every deal I handle, and the ones I can confirm from other brokers, I keep with the final price, not the asking price. It's what carries the most weight in a valuation, because a listing can lie and a closed sale can't.

No portal publishes closing prices systematically, so part of the job is simply asking around: colleagues, admin agents, whoever has recently bought a similar boat.

What the market discounts

Starting from a base price by model and year, there's a list of things an informed buyer will use to negotiate down, and rightly so:

  • High engine hours for the boat's age, or no recently documented reading.
  • A history with no invoices: services, antifouling, sails or electronics with no proof make the buyer assume the worst.
  • An expired ITB or close to expiring: the buyer has to sort it themselves, which costs time and money.
  • List 6 with fewer than 4 years on boats over 8 metres: it may carry an outstanding IEDMT if use switches to private. I cover this in detail in the guide to list 6 and list 7.
  • Foreign flag on a boat a Spanish resident will use: it needs to be nationalised, with cost and paperwork.
  • Mooring not included in areas where getting one takes months or years on a waiting list: it makes the boat less attractive, especially in high season.

Every one of these points is worth real money. They're not excuses for the buyer to haggle: they're costs or risks they'll be taking on after signing.

Rough depreciation guide

As a general industry reference, a well-maintained leisure boat typically loses around 30% of its value at 5 years and around 50% at 10 years, based on the new price. It's a rough figure, not a formula: a boat with excellent maintenance and low hours can depreciate less, and a neglected one, much more. Brand matters too: models with steady demand in Catalonia and the south of France (Beneteau, Jeanneau, Bavaria in both power and sail) hold their price better than brands less well known here.

Average time to sell, and the mistake of pricing too high

The average time to sell a used boat in the current market is around a year, and that figure already includes boats that went out overpriced and had to cut the price along the way. It's the pattern I see most: the seller sets a high price "to negotiate," the boat goes weeks without a single viewing, and by the time the price finally comes down it's already lost the interest of the first serious buyers, who saw it as overpriced and moved on.

A boat listed well at the right price moves fast, because buyers who've been watching the market for a while recognise a fair price straight away. Pricing high doesn't protect your margin: it shrinks it, because it drags out the time on the market and forces a later price cut, no longer with the pull of a fresh listing.

How I put together a valuation

I always follow the same process:

  1. I gather comparables of similar model and year, with closing price whenever I can confirm it, and listing price when I can't.
  2. I adjust for the boat's real condition: engine hours, history, ITB, list and registration, equipment, mooring.
  3. I give a range, not a single figure: an asking range, a bit above what I expect it to close at, to leave room to negotiate without putting anyone off; and a realistic closing range, which is what I use to tell the seller what they can expect.

I do this free of charge, using the details the seller sends me on WhatsApp: model, year, hours, condition and port. I usually have a first rough valuation within 24-48 hours.

Example with ranges

A 2021 Beneteau Flyer 8 Sundeck-type boat, with normal engine hours for its age, an invoiced history and mooring not included, typically moves in an asking range well above what it actually closes at. If similar comparables have closed, say, between €95,000 and €105,000, I'd advise asking around €108,000-112,000, not €130,000: the first gives room to negotiate without scaring anyone off; the second guarantees months without viewings. These figures are purely illustrative: every boat needs its own comparable.
