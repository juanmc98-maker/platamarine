Buying a boat in France or Italy and registering it in Spain: steps, taxes and when it's worth it

By Juan MoranteUpdated September 20268 min read

France and Italy carry far more used Beneteau and Jeanneau stock than Spain, sometimes with clear end-of-season discounts. Buying there and bringing the boat over can be worth it, but there are taxes and paperwork that often don't show up in the price you see in the listing. This guide walks through the process step by step and when it makes economic sense.

The steps in the process

  1. Check the boat's paperwork before committing to anything: ownership, original invoice with VAT shown, CE declaration of conformity and any outstanding charges.
  2. Settle whichever taxes apply in Spain: transfer tax if the seller is a private individual, VAT only if the boat counts as "new" for tax purposes, and IEDMT if it's over 8 metres. I explain this in detail in the next section.
  3. Deregister the boat from its country of origin's registry, where that country has a compulsory registry (not all do, see below).
  4. Prepare the registration file at the Spanish Harbour Master's Office: purchase contract or invoice, CE declaration of conformity, ID/NIE, tax receipts and the relevant fee.
  5. Get the Spanish Certificate of Registry and navigation permit, which is what lets you legally sail the boat once it's registered here.

Which taxes are due

This is where the three taxes get mixed up the most, so here they are separately:

  • Transfer tax (ITP): due if the seller is a private individual, set regionally, and in Catalonia the figure accountants usually work with is 5% of the boat's value (confirm case by case with your accountant, as it varies by region).
  • VAT: only due if the boat counts as "new" for tax purposes, meaning under 100 hours of use or less than 6 months since its first registration. A boat several years old with a normal usage history doesn't trigger this tax on a sale between private individuals.
  • IEDMT: 12% of the tax authority's table value, if the boat is over 8 metres in length and it's its first registration in Spain, even if it was already registered in another country. It's the same tax I cover in detail in the registration tax guide, and it works exactly the same way here.

The base for the IEDMT and the ITP doesn't always match what you paid: the tax authority has its own valuation tables and uses whichever is higher, the real price or the table value. It's worth asking your accountant for the exact calculation before closing the purchase, not after.

Country by country

France. The boat usually has an acte de francisation and a carte de circulation. You'll need the deregistration certificate (radiation) from the French registry, plus the invoice and proof that VAT has been paid.

Netherlands and Germany. Many boats under 12-15 metres aren't registered in any compulsory registry. There, proof of ownership is the chain of invoices: without the original invoice showing VAT separately, the sale gets a lot more complicated once it reaches Spain, because there's no simple way to prove the tax was paid back when it should have been.

Italy. The equivalent step is cancelling the registration (RID/ATCN), and you'll also need proof of VAT.

In every case, the original CE declaration of conformity is essential. If the boat doesn't have one, a post-construction assessment (EPF) is required, which carries a significant cost and adds quite a bit of time to the process.

Transport and the trip

On top of the taxes you need to budget for transport: usually by truck from the Côte d'Azur or Liguria to Barcelona, or sailing it yourself if you have the time and experience. Transport prices vary a lot depending on length and distance, so get a real quote before deciding; there's no fixed reference rate. On top of that comes the accountant's fee and, almost always, an inspection trip to see the boat in person and check it matches what was advertised.

Risks to bear in mind

  • No CE declaration: forces a post-construction assessment, adding cost and time.
  • No proof of VAT: nowadays one of the most common reasons a deal falls through, because without it the tax authority can demand it be paid again.
  • High table value: if the tax authority values the boat above what you paid, the IEDMT and the ITP are calculated on that higher figure, not on your purchase price.

On top of these risks there's the obvious one: buying without seeing the boat, or without someone you trust seeing it for you, is the most expensive mistake you can make. That's what the guide on what to check when buying a used boat is for.

The United Kingdom, briefly

Since Brexit, bringing a boat from the UK to Spain counts, for tax purposes, as an import from a third country, not a purchase within the EU. That means customs duty, 21% import VAT on the value plus duty and transport, the 12% IEDMT if it's over 8 metres, and you need to check the CE marking, because boats built after 2021 may only carry UKCA marking, which doesn't count in the EU and also requires a post-construction assessment. All told that's several procedures and costs stacking up, so it's only worth it if the discount in pounds is very large, or the boat is already in EU waters with EU VAT status proven.

When it's genuinely worth it

With all this on the table, the rule of thumb I apply is simple: importing pays off mainly in two cases.

  1. The boat is 8 metres or under, because then there's no IEDMT to pay, and the rest of the paperwork (ITP, deregistration, registration) is manageable.
  2. The price discount is generous, clearly above the 12% IEDMT plus transport, accountant's fees and the trip. If the saving compared to an equivalent boat in Spain is tight, the hidden costs of the process usually eat it up entirely.

For boats over 8 metres, I only recommend looking abroad if there's a very specific model you can't find here, or a price difference that comfortably absorbs the tax and the rest of the costs.

A worked example, with ranges

An 8.5-metre motorboat, priced noticeably lower in France than its equivalent in Spain, say an initial saving of €15,000-20,000 on price. On top of that boat you need to add: IEDMT (12% of the table value, which can run to several thousand euros), regional ITP if the seller is a private individual, transport (variable, get a quote), accountant's fees and the inspection trip. In many cases that whole set of costs cuts the initial saving in half or more. An equivalent 7.9-metre boat, on the other hand, saves the IEDMT outright, and the saving stays far more intact. These figures are illustrative: each case needs calculating with real data on the boat and a transport quote.
