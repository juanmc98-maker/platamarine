Boat registration tax: why 8 metres changes everything

By Juan MoranteUpdated September 20267 min read

If you've been looking at used boats you'll have noticed that a lot of them measure exactly 7.99 metres. That's no coincidence: from 8 metres of length upward, first registration in Spain carries a 12% tax. This guide explains what that tax is, when it's due, how much it costs and how it affects the price of a used boat.

What the IEDMT is

The registration tax is officially called the Special Tax on Certain Means of Transport (IEDMT). It's the same tax paid when registering a car, and in boating it applies to leisure boats over 8 metres in length when they're registered in Spain for the first time (or when a resident of Spain uses one here, even under a foreign flag).

It's administered by the Spanish Tax Agency, settled using form 576 before registration, and without that receipt the Harbour Master's Office won't register the boat. Personal watercraft always pay it, whatever their length.

How much is due

The general rate is 12% of the boat's value. For a new boat the base is the purchase price excluding VAT; for a used or imported one, the base is market value, which the tax authority calculates using its official boat valuation tables (an order published every year), or the real price if it's higher.

On the hull, or on the whole boat? On the whole boat, as it's registered: hull, engine and all equipment fitted and invoiced with it (electronics, canopies, bow thruster, and so on). An example: an 8.5-metre boat that costs €50,000 for the hull alone and €80,000 with engine and electronics pays 12% on the €80,000 (€9,600), not on the €50,000. The only things left out are what isn't part of the boat itself, like a trailer, or accessories bought and fitted later with their own separate invoice. And watch out for one more thing: the tax authority's tables often come out higher than the price you negotiated, and if the real price is higher than the table, the real price wins. Before buying a boat over 8 metres that has never been registered in Spain, it's worth asking your accountant for the exact calculation.

A worked example

Two similar boats, both from 2019 and worth around €85,000. The first measures 7.99 m: registering it in Spain for the first time carries no IEDMT, just an exemption declaration (form 06). The second measures 8.50 m: it pays 12% of whatever value the tax authority sets, around €10,200 if the table values it at €85,000. That difference explains why Beneteau, Jeanneau or Quicksilver design so many models just under 8 metres.

When it's due, and when it isn't

It's paid only once, on first registration in Spain. A 9-metre boat bought new from a Spanish dealer already has the tax paid; when that boat is later sold second-hand between private individuals, the buyer doesn't pay IEDMT again. What's due instead is transfer tax (ITP), a different tax set regionally.

It is due when a boat enters the Spanish registry for the first time, even if it's used. The typical cases:

  • A boat over 8 metres bought in France, Italy or Germany and registered in Spain.
  • A foreign-flagged boat that a resident of Spain regularly uses in Spanish waters.
  • A boat that was exempt because it was used for charter (list 6) and changes use early. This case deserves a section of its own.

The exemptions that matter

Boats up to 8 metres

They don't pay, but the exemption isn't automatic: form 06 has to be filed with the Tax Agency before registration. The length that counts is the one shown in the boat's paperwork (the hull length on the CE declaration of conformity), not the overall length including the bathing platform or pulpits.

Boats used for charter (list 6)

A boat over 8 metres used exclusively for charter can be registered exempt from IEDMT. The exemption is requested with form 05 and granted by the tax authority before registration. In exchange, the boat must be on list 6, registered as a business activity and carry the insurance, inspections and equipment required of a commercial vessel.

Here's the trap that causes the most trouble on the used market: the law requires the exemption's conditions to be kept up for 4 years. If before that period the boat leaves charter use or is sold to a private individual who wants it for private use, the IEDMT must be settled at that point, on the boat's value at that time. A 10-metre ex-charter boat only three years old can be hiding an €8,000 or €10,000 bill that the seller doesn't always mention.

How it affects the price of a used boat

In practice, when I value a boat for a client I check three things related to this tax:

  1. Real length on the paperwork. 7.99 m and 8.10 m look the same on the pontoon and they aren't to the tax authority.
  2. Whether it's already registered in Spain. If it is and it's on list 7, the tax is history and doesn't affect the deal.
  3. If it comes from list 6, when it was registered. Over 4 years, no problem; under that, the price has to discount the outstanding tax or the deal makes no sense for the buyer.

That's why 7.5 to 8-metre outboard boats are some of the most liquid on the market: they can be imported, change hands and be resold without this tax getting in the way. And that's why a 9 or 10-metre boat registered in Spain on list 7, with the IEDMT already paid, carries extra value compared to the same model under a foreign flag that a Spanish buyer would have to nationalise.
