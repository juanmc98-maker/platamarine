What paperwork you need to sell your boat and how much the change of ownership costs

By Juan MoranteUpdated September 20267 min read

Most sales that fall through don't fall through over price. They fall through because a document is missing, or because the buyer finds a charge on the registry sheet halfway through the deal. Here's the full list of documents, who pays what, the deadlines you need to respect, and a real calculation of what changing ownership costs.

The documents you need

Gather these before you advertise the boat. Better to find out now if something's missing than at signing:

  • Private purchase contract, with the identity of both parties, the boat's name, registration number, hull identification number (HIN), engine make and serial number, price, date, declared condition, an attached inventory, and a clear clause on charges. An incomplete contract is the most common mistake I see, and the one that causes the most trouble later.
  • National ID or foreign resident ID for buyer and seller.
  • Up-to-date registry sheet (hoja de asiento). Requested from the Harbour Master's office, it's where liens, charges and maritime mortgages are recorded. Always request it, even if you trust the seller: it's the only way to know if the boat is genuinely free of debt.
  • Spanish Registry Certificate and navigation permit, both current.
  • Current ITB (Technical Boat Inspection), the equivalent of a roadworthiness test for boats.
  • EC declaration of conformity, for boats built after 1998, and a ship radio station licence if it carries a radio.
  • Invoices and manuals for the equipment, especially if the electronics or engine are recent.

If the boat is over 8 metres and comes from list 6 with fewer than 4 years since registration, there may be an outstanding registration tax (IEDMT) when it moves to private use. I cover this in detail in the guide to list 6 and list 7.

Transfer tax (ITP): paid by the buyer, and it depends on where they live

The Property Transfer Tax (ITP) applies to a private sale of a boat already registered in Spain. The buyer pays it, calculated on the contract price or on the value in the tax authority's official tables, whichever is higher, unless a lower value can be justified.

In Catalonia the applicable rate is 5% of that base, filed with form 620. Worth being clear about: this rate depends on the buyer's autonomous community of residence, not on where the boat is. Each region sets its own rate, so before closing a deal with a buyer from outside Catalonia, confirm the rate that applies to them with an admin agent or the relevant regional tax office.

The deadline to pay the ITP is 30 working days from signing the contract. There are surcharges once that deadline passes.

The Harbour Master's fee

Besides the ITP, the change of ownership carries an administrative fee: form 790, code 025, from the Boat Registry. It's calculated roughly on the boat's tonnage (GT), and for most leisure boats in this segment it comes to around €20. It's a small amount, but the file can't be processed without proof of payment.

The 3-month deadline

From the date of sale you have 3 months to file the change of ownership with the Harbour Master's office of the port of registration (for boats on list 7, it can be filed at any Harbour Master's office). There's a penalty once that deadline passes. The Harbour Master's office usually takes 3 to 4 weeks to resolve it; in the meantime, the admin agent can normally issue a provisional receipt that lets you sail with the file already submitted.

Don't sail without that receipt. A boat that's been sold but has no change of ownership filed is still, technically, sailing under the name and responsibility of whoever is on the registry.

How much the admin agent costs

The cost of an admin agent (gestoría) for a standard change of ownership usually runs between €100 and €250, as a rough guide, depending on the agent and how complex the file is. As a market reference, one online agent (Tramitfy) publishes a fee of €154.99, taxes included, for a straightforward change of ownership. Prices vary by agent and whether there are complications (charges to resolve, a simultaneous list change, an incomplete file), so get a fixed quote before starting.

Example with numbers

A boat worth €80,000 sold between private individuals, buyer resident in Catalonia. The ITP at 5% comes to €4,000, paid by the buyer. Add the Harbour Master's fee, around €20, and the admin agent, say €180 in the middle of the range. In total, the buyer faces just over €4,200 on top of the boat's price in paperwork alone, not counting the insurance they must have from the day of delivery. This is a calculation I always give before anyone makes an offer, because it changes the real budget for the deal.

Insurance, from the day of delivery

Third-party liability insurance is mandatory for every motor boat, and for sailing boats without an engine over 6 metres, with a minimum cover set by law. The new owner must have insurance in place from the moment the boat is delivered, not from when the change of ownership is finalised. Sailing between delivery and the file being resolved without insurance is a risk that isn't worth taking to save a day's paperwork.

Common mistakes I see

  • Sailing before starting the change of ownership process.
  • Not requesting the registry sheet and finding out about a charge or lien after already being paid.
  • Letting the 3-month deadline for the change of ownership or the 30-day deadline for the ITP pass, with the corresponding surcharge.
  • Setting the ITP only on the agreed price without checking whether the tax office's tables give a higher value.
  • An incomplete contract: no HIN, no engine details, no inventory, no clause on charges.
  • Not checking the ITB is current before signing.
  • Buying an ex-charter boat over 8 metres with fewer than 4 years on list 6 without budgeting for the outstanding IEDMT.
  • Confusing the ITP (private sale), VAT (sale by a company) and the IEDMT (first registration): three different taxes that don't overlap, but each applies to a specific case.

A well-written contract, with an inventory and declared condition, also protects the seller: in Spain a buyer can claim for hidden defects under warranties that run 12-24 months depending on the case, so putting the boat's real condition at the time of sale in writing isn't extra paperwork, it's your backup if something is claimed later.
