The ITB inspection for leisure boats: what it is, who needs it and how often

By Juan MoranteSeptember 20268 min read

The ITB is to boats what the ITV (MOT) is to cars, with two differences that trip a lot of people up: not every boat has to pass it, and it isn't done at a testing station — an inspector comes to the harbour or the boatyard. My own boat is under 6 metres and doesn't need it, but as a broker I check it on every deal, because an expired certificate becomes the buyer's problem from the day they sign. Here's what you need to know, without the jargon.

What the ITB is

The technical inspection of boats is the set of surveys Spanish law requires for leisure boats to confirm they're still safe: hull, engine, electrical system, fuel, safety and life-saving equipment, lights, bilge pumps. It's governed by Royal Decree 1434/1999 and covers Spanish-flagged leisure boats between 2.5 and 24 metres of hull length.

The administration doesn't carry it out directly. It's done by authorised inspection bodies (ECIs), private companies approved by the Directorate-General of the Merchant Marine, and the result is the certificate of seaworthiness, which must be kept on board. The inspector comes to the harbour or the boatyard: there's no need to take the boat anywhere unless it has to be seen hauled out.

Which boats need it, and which are exempt

What decides whether your boat needs the ITB and how often comes down to two things: the list it's registered on (list 7, private use; list 6, charter) and the hull length, which is the figure shown in the paperwork, not the overall length including the bathing platform.

  • List 7 and under 6 metres: no periodic inspections. The certificate of seaworthiness is issued with no expiry date. This covers most opens, RIBs and trailerable boats.
  • List 7, 6 to 24 metres: a periodic inspection at most every 5 years. Boats of 15 metres or more, and wooden-hulled boats of 6 metres or more, also have an intermediate inspection between the second and third year.
  • List 6 (charter), any length: periodic every 5 years and intermediate between the second and third year from 6 metres up. A boat coming out of charter usually has the most complete inspection history, which is an advantage when buying it.

Watch for 5.99 and 7.99

The same way boats cluster just under 8 metres for the registration tax, they cluster just under 6 for the ITB: many boats are certified with a hull length of 5.95 or 5.99 metres precisely to avoid periodic inspections. If in doubt, check the CE declaration of conformity or the registration certificate; that's the length that counts.

What the inspector checks

The official list is long, but on a 6 to 12-metre motorboat what really decides the outcome is this:

  • Hull and underwater hull (hauled out): general condition, seacocks and through-hull fittings, anodes, propeller and shaft or leg, rudder. This is where the surprises come from: a seized seacock or a spent anode is enough for a defect note.
  • Watertightness: hatches, portlights, companionways, exhaust outlet, and that the boat doesn't take on water.
  • Engine and fuel: mounts, exhaust, tank and fuel hoses (date and condition), compartment ventilation on petrol engines, an accessible fuel shut-off.
  • Electrical system: batteries secured and protected, a main isolator, wiring without visible splices, protection devices.
  • Gas, if there's a galley: cylinder in a locker vented outboard, shut-off valve, approved piping.
  • Bilge pumps and firefighting: working bilge pumps (manual and electric), in-date extinguishers with the capacity required for the boat's length.
  • Safety and life-saving equipment: lifejackets, lifebuoys, in-date flares, liferaft where the zone requires one, navigation lights, horn, compass, and whatever the certificate's navigation zone requires (Royal Decree 339/2021).
  • Radio: VHF with a ship station licence, if fitted.
  • Documentation: registration certificate, previous certificate, insurance, and that the painted name and registration number match the paperwork.

If something fails, the inspector notes the defects in writing and sets a deadline to fix them and be checked again. It's usually not dramatic: the most common issues are expired extinguishers or flares, an old fuel hose, or a stiff seacock.

How it's done and what it costs

You call an authorised inspection body (there are several along the whole Catalan coast; the club or boatyard will tell you which one they work with), set a date, and if it's the periodic inspection, time it to coincide with the annual haul-out so you don't pay for the crane twice. The inspector checks the boat, verifies the safety equipment and, if everything's in order, issues the certificate of seaworthiness with the date of the next inspection. It's worth having in-date extinguishers and flares and the boat's paperwork ready beforehand: that's what most often forces a second visit.

The cost depends on the length and the inspection body; it's paid to the body, not the administration. For an 8 to 12-metre boat it's a few hundred euros every 5 years, plus the haul-out if you weren't planning one anyway. It's a small part of the cost of owning a boat, and far cheaper than discovering a hull problem while out sailing.

What happens if it lapses

Without a valid certificate of seaworthiness the boat can't legally sail: it's a serious offence under the State Ports and Merchant Marine Law, with a fine and possible impoundment if you're stopped for an inspection. And there's another consequence people don't see coming: after a claim, the insurer can dispute cover if the boat wasn't legally fit to sail. Getting it done on time costs less than either of those.

The ITB when buying a used boat

It's one of the first things I ask for when I handle a sale, and I'd recommend doing the same if you're buying on your own:

  • Ask for the certificate of seaworthiness and check two dates: the last inspection and the next one due. If less than 6 months are left, plan on getting it done yourself soon.
  • If it's expired, the boat shouldn't be sailing; negotiate for the seller to renew it before handover or deduct it from the price, and be wary of "it's just paperwork".
  • Check the defect notes on the last report, if there were any: they say a lot about how the boat's been looked after.
  • If the boat's had an engine change or a major repair, there should be an additional inspection on record. If there isn't, the change isn't reflected in the paperwork, and that becomes your problem.
  • A boat under 6 metres has no periodic ITB, so the check is on you: it matters even more to see the engine running and the hull hauled out.
  • An ex-charter boat (list 6) comes with an inspection history every 2-3 years. Looked at the right way, that's a point in its favour; I cover it in the list 6 and 7 guide.

Frequently asked questions

Does my boat under 6 metres need to pass the ITB?

If it's on list 7 (private use) and its hull length is under 6 metres, it has no periodic inspections: its certificate of seaworthiness is issued with no expiry date. It's still required to carry the safety equipment for its navigation zone and to undergo an additional inspection if the engine is changed or it suffers serious damage.

How often is the ITB done on a leisure boat?

On list 7, at 6 metres or more, the periodic inspection is required at most every 5 years. On list 6 (charter) it's also every 5 years, with an intermediate inspection between the second and third year. List 7 boats of 15 metres or more, and wooden-hulled boats of 6 metres or more, also have an intermediate inspection.

Is the ITB done with the boat in the water or hauled out?

The periodic inspection includes checking the hull hauled out, so the boat needs to be lifted out, or the inspection timed to coincide with the maintenance haul-out. Intermediate inspections can be done afloat.

What happens if I buy a boat with an expired ITB?

Sailing without a valid certificate of seaworthiness is a serious offence, and the insurer can also raise problems over a claim. Before buying, ask for the certificate, check the date of the next inspection, and if it's expired, deduct it from the price or agree that the seller will pass it before handover.
