Boat valuation and survey: what each one is and when you need it

By Juan MoranteUpdated October 20265 min read

“Valuing a boat” is used for three different things: knowing what to ask, knowing what condition it is in, and having a report that a bank, insurer or court will accept. Mixing them up can be costly, so here they are one by one.

Three things that get mixed up

  • Market valuation. Answers “how much can I ask or pay?”. It is done by comparing with similar boats being listed and sold. It is what a broker gives you, or the how much is my boat worth calculator.
  • Survey (inspection). Answers “what condition is it really in?”. A surveyor checks the boat, usually out of the water and with a sea trial, and gives you a condition report.
  • Formal valuation. A report signed by a surveyor that sets a value and is accepted by third parties: finance, insurance, inheritance, division of assets or a court case.

When you need each one

  • You are selling. A well-made market valuation is usually enough to set the asking price.
  • You are buying. A survey is what protects you most, especially on larger boats or boats with inboard engines. Have it done before paying, or make the deposit conditional on the result; I explain it in the guide to deposits (arras).
  • You are financing or insuring. Some banks and insurers ask for a valuation or condition report, especially on older boats. Ask first which format they accept.
  • Inheritance, gift or divorce. For tax purposes the tax value from the official tables is used; if the boat is clearly worth less, a surveyor's valuation helps to justify it.

Who does it

An independent marine surveyor. When buying, the key point is that they are not paid out of the sale: neither the seller nor the intermediary should choose the surveyor for you. Ask beforehand what they will check, whether a sea trial and haul-out are included, and how long the report takes.

What a good survey checks

  • Hull and deck: impacts, repairs, gelcoat cracks and moisture in the laminate (with a meter).
  • Engines and drivetrain: hours, leaks, cooling, sterndrives or shafts and, ideally, an oil analysis.
  • Electrical system, gas, fuel, bilge pumps and seacocks.
  • Rigging and sails, if it is a sailboat.
  • Safety equipment and paperwork: that what the documents say matches the boat.

Online valuations

Online calculators, mine included, are useful to get your bearings: they tell you the range similar boats are listed at. They don't see your boat, so they don't replace a survey before buying, or a formal valuation when someone requires one.

Frequently asked questions

Is a survey compulsory before buying a used boat?

It isn't compulsory, but it is strongly advisable, especially if the boat is older, has inboard engines or a high price. A hidden problem usually costs far more than the survey.

Who pays for the survey?

Usually whoever commissions it, normally the buyer. If the bank or insurer asks for it, the customer usually pays as well.

Can I use a broker's valuation as a formal valuation?

It is useful for setting or negotiating the price. If a bank, insurer or court asks for a formal valuation, you will need a report signed by a surveyor.
