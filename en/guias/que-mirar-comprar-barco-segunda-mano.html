What to check when buying a used boat from 7 to 12 metres

By Juan MoranteUpdated September 20268 min read

A boat that looks great in photos can be hiding a worn-out engine, a registry sheet with charges on it, or a length that doesn't match what the tax office has on record. Here's what I check, in this order, every time someone asks me to go over a boat before they make an offer.

Paperwork, before anything else

Before stepping on board, I ask for the paperwork. A boat without clean documentation isn't worth what the listing says, no matter how nice the upholstery.

  • Up-to-date registry sheet, requested from the Harbour Master's office. It's where charges, liens and maritime mortgages are recorded. Without this there's no deal, it's the first filter.
  • Registration list (6 or 7). If it's list 6 (charter) and has been registered fewer than 4 years, there may be an outstanding IEDMT if it moves to private use. I cover this in detail in the guide to list 6 and list 7.
  • The exact length on the EC declaration of conformity, not what the seller says from memory. Between 7.99 m and 8.10 m there's a tax at stake; I cover it in the guide to the IEDMT and the 8-metre threshold.
  • Current ITB (technical inspection), seaworthiness certificate and EC declaration if built after 1998.
  • Maintenance and equipment invoices. A seller who keeps them is, almost always, a seller who's looked after the boat.

The full change-of-ownership process, deadlines and taxes is covered in detail in paperwork to sell a boat and change of ownership.

The engine: the biggest factor in the decision

The engine is the part that costs the most to fix and shows the least in photos. I check:

  • Engine hours and whether they match the year. As a rough guide, a well-cared-for engine in leisure use usually runs between 50 and 100 hours a year; a lot more hours for the boat's age calls for an explanation (charter, heavy use), and a lot fewer does too, because an engine left idle for long periods develops its own problems.
  • Cold start. I ask to see it start without having been run earlier that day. Excessive smoke, a metallic knock, or a slow start are red flags.
  • Service history: oil changes, filters, anodes, belts, servicing of the shaft line or the outboard's lower unit.
  • Outboard or inboard. An outboard is easier and cheaper to inspect and replace, and it dominates on the 7-9 metre boats in this area. Inboards matter more on larger and displacement boats, with different maintenance (shaft line, cooling, exhaust system).
  • Make and reliability. There's no bad brand as such, but some have a bigger network of workshops and parts in Catalonia, which matters the day something fails. Yamaha, Suzuki and Mercury have a good reputation for reliability in outboards; Volvo Penta and Yanmar are the most common inboards in the area, with specialist workshops in Barcelona and Girona.

Hull and structure

The hull can't be fixed with a nice photo. I check, with the boat out of the water if possible:

  • Osmosis: bubbles or blisters in the gelcoat below the waterline. Not always serious, but it changes the price and needs budgeting for.
  • Impact damage and poorly finished repairs, especially on the bow and the transom.
  • Bilge: dry and clean is a good sign. Water, rust or traces of oil, and you need to ask why.
  • Through-hull fittings and valves: that they turn freely and show no corrosion.

Example with numbers

Take a 9-metre Jeanneau from 2016 with 480 engine hours. For its age that's a reasonable figure (about 60 hours a year). If the seller has invoices for regular servicing and the bilge is dry and odourless, a 40-minute sea trial can be enough to decide: the paperwork and visual check together already give confidence. The same boat with 900 hours and not a single invoice is a different story: that's where I'd ask for a survey before making an offer. A report that reveals pending engine work (a head gasket, a sloppy sail-drive) is worth what it costs, because it justifies a price cut that often comfortably covers the cost of the survey.

Electronics and equipment

I check that everything turns on and works with the engine running, not just on shore power: GPS/plotter, depth sounder, autopilot, VHF, navigation lights, automatic bilge pump, and start and house batteries separately. Old equipment isn't a problem if the price already reflects it; the problem is equipment the seller says works that never gets tested.

Sea trial and independent survey

A sea trial is mandatory in my process, not optional. We run at different engine speeds, test turning, trim, how the engine behaves once warm, and listen for vibration or unusual noise.

For boats worth roughly €60,000-80,000 or more, or when something in the visual check doesn't add up, I recommend an independent survey. The buyer pays for it, it's a rough figure (it varies by surveyor and boat size), and its purpose is to give a neutral technical report: hull, engine, electrics, safety. It's the same logic as the survey in the English-speaking market, where it's almost always part of the process before signing.

Warning signs

  • The seller doesn't have, or won't show, the registry sheet.
  • Engine hours that don't match the visible wear on controls, seats or deck.
  • A price well below market with no clear explanation.
  • An ex-charter boat (list 6) registered fewer than 4 years with no mention of an outstanding IEDMT.
  • Reluctance to allow a sea trial or a third party (survey) to inspect the boat.

How to negotiate with data

Negotiation that works isn't "knock 5,000 off just because," it's negotiating with concrete data: engine hours above average for the year, a visible hull repair, equipment that doesn't work, something a survey picks up. Each point has an estimable cost, and that cost is the argument. Comparing against real closing prices for similar boats, not listing prices, also helps: asking prices are almost always above what actually ends up being paid.
